Lesson 4.9 — Soft edges and antialiasing

Lesson 4.8 gave every mask position an alpha value. This lesson asks what alpha should be used when a mathematical shape boundary passes through only part of one pixel-sized area.

Visible problem first: a hard whole-pixel decision makes a diagonal or curved edge look like a staircase. Antialiasing replaces that harsh staircase with a narrow transition whose pixels keep different fractions of the shape.

One new idea

Coverage is the fraction of one pixel-sized area occupied by the shape in the controlled teaching model.

The lesson traces coverage into alpha, then into the already-known compositing operation.

1 · Objects before formulas

Separate the boundary, the grid, and the output pixels

They are three different objects.

Mathematical boundary

An infinitely thin line that describes where the shape ends. It has no pixel thickness by itself.

Pixel grid

A rectangular set of pixel-sized areas. Each output position must receive one stored result.

Rendered edge

The narrow group of output pixels whose values represent the boundary on that grid.

Important: the geometric boundary is not the same object as the rendered transition band. The renderer must convert the first object into the second.

8 · Why partial coverage works — and where sampling stops being exact

The alpha is useful only because it carries geometric information

A lower alpha is not automatically prettier. The useful value is the coverage estimate for that exact position.

Hard whole-pixel decisions

Every boundary position chooses only fully inside or fully outside. A diagonal or curve becomes a visible staircase.

Coverage-aware boundary pixels

The intermediate pixels encode where the mathematical edge crosses the grid. The eye reads the narrow transition as a smoother edge.

Not arbitrary softness: setting every edge to alpha 0.60 would blur or weaken the silhouette. Each boundary position needs its own coverage value, such as 0.18, 0.60, or 0.91, derived from the geometry at that position.

The same coverage question works for lines, curves, waves, text, and arbitrary paths

The renderer asks one local question at each affected output position: what fraction of this pixel-sized area belongs to the shape? The boundary can be straight or curved. The meaning of the resulting coverage value stays the same.

What the thin-band example is

The orange object is a narrow band. It has a top boundary and a bottom boundary. Only the area between those two boundaries belongs to the shape.

It therefore does not divide the pixel into one orange half and one outside half. Positions above the band and below the band are both outside.

What one teaching sample is

Each faint square is an equal-area subcell. The tiny pin at its center is the one coordinate tested by the shortcut.

The visible marker is enlarged so you can see it. A marker circle touching orange does not count. Only its center pin decides whether that entire subcell casts an inside or outside vote.

Finite-sampling limit: sample points are probes, not knives that split the pixel into exact geometric pieces. Each center point gives one yes-or-no vote for its equal-area subcell. A narrow band can cross a subcell while missing its center. More points usually reduce the error, but any finite point pattern can still miss a sufficiently small feature.

A partial result can still be only an estimate

The next graphic uses one straight edge. The true orange area is 40% of the pixel. A 2 × 2 center-sample shortcut detects partial coverage, but its two inside votes report 50%. The result is partial, yet still not the exact split.

1 · Actual geometric split

left covered height = 65% right covered height = 15% coverage = average covered height = (65% + 15%) ÷ 2 = 40%

2 · Two-by-two sample shortcut

inside center pins = 2 total center pins = 4 estimated coverage = 2 ÷ 4 = 50%

The shortcut noticed a partial pixel. It still overestimated the real 40% area.

3 · Mitigation without more samples

Analytic coverage means calculating from the edge geometry directly instead of estimating from center-point votes. For this straight edge, the renderer can find where the edge crosses the pixel sides and measure the enclosed area.

analytic edge–pixel intersection → covered area 40% → no extra center samples

A curved edge needs math that follows the curve or estimates distance from it. Compose and Android's renderer own that work. The Liquid Glass app normally supplies the Path; it does not implement a rasterizer.

Conceptual contract

geometry at position (x, y) → coverage between 0 and 1 → shape alpha × coverage → source-over destination

This result contract applies regardless of the internal rasterization method.

What the 20 × 20 grid proves

It proves the teaching arithmetic and makes partial area visible. It does not prove that Android stores 400 sub-pixel samples for every physical pixel.

3 · Diagnostic: does antialiasing blur neighboring colors?

Does the coverage calculation copy orange from a neighboring pixel?

Show answer

No. The controlled coverage calculation determines the contribution of the shape at the current position. Blur is a different operation that reads neighboring image positions.

4 · Diagnostic: the sample marker touches the thin band

A large white marker circle visually overlaps the orange band, but its tiny center pin is outside. Does that sample count as inside?

Show answer

No. The shortcut tests the center coordinate only. The marker circle is enlarged for visibility and has no geometric area in the calculation. This is why the revised graphic draws the center pin separately from the equal-area subcell it represents.
