Chapter 9 · Measure a diagonal with a right triangle

Distance between two points

Question: Lesson 4.1 already named a circle’s radius: the straight-line length from the center to the boundary. That lesson measured the radius only along a horizontal line. Lesson 8.8 already built an extra pair p = uv − float2(0.5, 0.5) so a pixel can be numbered from the visual center. How do you compute the straight-line length of p when this pixel is not on a horizontal or vertical line from the center?

Draw a right triangle first: a triangle with one square corner, like the corner of a rectangle. The two sides that meet at that corner are the legs. Their lengths are the horizontal difference and the vertical difference. The remaining side is the long side: the straight-line run between the two points. For this lesson’s teaching pixel, those legs are 0.30 and 0.40. The long side is 0.50. Metal’s name for that number is length(p).
Complete. This page is a JavaScript stand-in. The iPhone 16 Pro explorer’s stitchable function computes length(p). Lesson 9.2 subtracts a radius so a minus can mean inside. smoothstep around a boundary is Lesson 9.3. Lesson 8.8’s optional × 2 is unused.

1 · THE NEW QUESTION

A horizontal radius cannot measure a diagonal pixel.

4.1 leftover

Lesson 4.1 defined a circle from one center and one radius. Radius meant the straight-line length from the center to the boundary. That lesson’s radius handle moved only horizontally, on purpose, so it did not need this formula.

8.8 leftover

Lesson 8.8 built p so tests can ask “which way from the middle?” The visual-center pixel still has UV (0.5, 0.5). Its p is (0, 0). The draw rectangle does not move. This lesson reads both members of p together.

New length

The teaching pixel sits at p = (0.30, −0.40). It is right of center and in the top half. Neither 0.30 alone nor −0.40 alone is the straight-line length from the center to that pixel.

Not a sign yet

The length this lesson stores is never negative. A minus on p.y is only “up from the center” in y-down coordinates. Lesson 9.2 will subtract a radius from this length. That later subtract can produce a negative number. × 2 from Lesson 8.8 is unused: the teaching 0.30 and 0.40 stay in the subtract-only pair.

Why the rectangle is square. UV divides x by width and y by height. If the rectangle is 400 × 160, one UV step right is 400 pixels and one UV step down is 160 pixels. Then 0.30 in p.x and 0.40 in p.y would not be the same kind of length. This lesson’s lab is 400 × 400 so one UV step right equals one UV step down in pixels. The iPhone 16 Pro explorer is 360 pt × 360 pt for the same reason.
2 · COUNTABLE TRIANGLE FIRST

A 3-by-4 right triangle has a long side of 5.

Count the unit squares. Do not start from a named theorem. The shader numbers later are this same triangle scaled by one tenth.

What you can count

The 3-square has 9 cells. The 4-square has 16 cells. 9 + 16 = 25. The 5-square has 25 cells. For a right triangle, the square built on the long side has the same area as the two squares built on the legs added together.

3 × 3 + 4 × 4 = 9 + 16 = 25 = 5 × 5 long side = 5

Same triangle, one tenth as large

Divide every side by 10. The shape does not change. The teaching pixel uses this smaller copy: legs 0.30 and 0.40, long side 0.50. You can check 0.50 without a calculator: 0.50 × 0.50 = 0.25, and 0.09 + 0.16 = 0.25.

0.30 × 0.30 = 0.09 0.40 × 0.40 = 0.16 0.09 + 0.16 = 0.25 0.50 × 0.50 = 0.25 so the long side is 0.50

Some textbooks call this area rule the Pythagorean theorem, and they call the long side the hypotenuse. You do not need those names to use the arithmetic. This course will say right triangle, legs, and long side.

3 · SQUARE ROOT

Undo the square: find the number that multiplies by itself

A square of a number is that number multiplied by itself. A square root of a number is a value whose square equals that number. Distance is a length, so this course uses the square root that is zero or greater: sqrt(25) = 5, not −5. Metal’s name is sqrt.

The area rule as a formula

Call the horizontal leg dx and the vertical leg dy. The long side is d. The countable picture said d × d = dx × dx + dy × dy. Taking the square root that is zero or greater of both sides gives d.

dx = 0.30 dy = −0.40 dx × dx = 0.09 dy × dy = (−0.40) × (−0.40) = 0.16 sum = 0.25 d = sqrt(0.25) = 0.50 ← 0.50 × 0.50 = 0.25 is the check

The minus does not survive

p.y = −0.40 means the pixel is above the center. A negative times a negative is positive, so the vertical square still has area 0.16. The long side is 0.50, not −0.50. length never returns a minus. If you need “inside versus outside,” that is a later subtract in Lesson 9.2, not a property of this square root.

The primary reason for squaring is the area rule, not “to delete the sign.” Deleting the sign is a side effect of the same multiply.

Why the gray is a circle in one picture and a diamond in the other

Your reading of the circle picture is correct, with one extra precision. The picture is not one disk painted a single gray. Every pixel stores its own number. All pixels that share one number sit on one equal-value outline. For Straight-line gray that number is length(p). All pixels at one length sit on a circle. A smaller length is a smaller circle, and that circle is darker because the stored number is closer to 0 (black). A larger length is a larger circle, and that circle is a lighter gray because the stored number is farther from 0. The center stores 0, so it is black. This square’s corners store 0.707, so they stay fairly dark.

Walk-around gray uses the same stacking rule with a different number. The analog of “a circle of this length” is “a diamond of this walk-around number.” The walk-around number is abs(p.x) + abs(p.y): how far you travel from the center if you may move only horizontally and then only vertically. All pixels that share one walk-around number sit on one diamond. A smaller walk-around number is a smaller diamond, and it is darker. A larger walk-around number is a larger diamond, and it is lighter. The inner diamond of walk-around 0.25 is to the diamond picture what the inner circle of length 0.25 is to the circle picture.

Circle: smaller radius is darker

Yes. Pixels on a circle of radius 0.10 all store 0.10, near black. Pixels on a circle of radius 0.25 all store 0.25. Pixels on the teaching circle of radius 0.50 all store 0.50. That 0.50 circle is the largest full circle that still fits in this square: it touches the four edge-middles. The four corners store 0.707, lighter than 0.50, still not white. There is no fifth full circle drawn through those corners, because that circle would sit outside the square except at the corners themselves.

The picture looks like a dark circular blob because those circles are stacked. Each circle is a little lighter than the one inside it. It is not one disk filled with a single gray.

Diamond: the analog of a smaller-radius circle

A smaller-radius circle is every pixel with a smaller length(p). The analog in Walk-around gray is every pixel with a smaller walk-around number abs(p.x) + abs(p.y).

That set is a smaller diamond, and it is darker, for the same gray contract: 0 is black. The inner diamond of 0.25 is the analog of the inner circle of 0.25. The diamond of 0.50 goes through the four edge-middles: that is the largest full diamond inside this square. The teaching pixel’s diamond is 0.70; its vertices sit outside the square, so you see only the part the square still contains. The four corners store 1.00 and go white. There is no fifth full diamond drawn through those corners inside the picture, because that diamond would sit outside the square except at the corners themselves.

Yes: the diamond is a square rotated 45 degrees

It is a square standing on a vertex. Its four vertices point up, right, down, and left, along the axes. They do not point at the teaching square’s corners.

Take walk-around 0.50. One pixel that stores 0.50 is the right-edge middle, p = (0.50, 0), because 0.50 + 0 = 0.50. Another is the top-edge middle, p = (0, −0.50), because 0 + 0.50 = 0.50. From the right-edge middle to the top-edge middle you go left 0.50 and up 0.50. That is a 45-degree line. The next side goes left 0.50 and down 0.50 to the left-edge middle. Those two directions meet like the corner of a rectangle, so each corner of this outline is a square corner. Four equal sides with square corners make a square. It is rotated 45 degrees relative to the teaching square around it.

This course still says diamond so that outline is not confused with the axis-aligned teaching square. A walk-around of 0.50 can reach an edge-middle. It cannot reach a corner: a corner is 0.50 + 0.50 = 1.00. A circle of length 0.50 also hits the edge-middles, then continues farther toward the corners, up to 0.707.

Why 0.70 is larger than 0.50

        
Both numbers join the same two pixels: the visual center and the teaching pixel. Walk-around is the path that is forced to pass through the amber right-angle point. You pay the full 0.30 and then the full 0.40. The long side is the one remaining side of that triangle. It goes from the center to the teaching pixel without stopping at the amber point. For any right triangle, the two legs added together are longer than the remaining side. That is the whole reason 0.70 drops to 0.50 when you switch from walk-around to length. No extra operation is hiding in the phrase “shorter path.” You simply did not travel via the amber point.

      

      

        
What walk-around is for, in this course

        
You already have two members, 0.30 and 0.40. Adding them is the first combination most people try: “this pixel is 0.30 right and 0.40 up, so 0.70 away.” That addition is a real length. It answers: if you may move only horizontally and then only vertically, how far do you travel? Equal values of that length form a diamond, a square standing on a vertex. Section 4’s Walk-around gray button paints that diamond. The nested-outline figure under the scale shows the smaller diamonds inside it. Those smaller diamonds are the analog of smaller-radius circles.

        
A circle needs a different question: the same straight-line length from the center in every direction, including diagonals. Walk-around is smaller along the axes than toward the corners, so it cannot describe a circle. Lesson 4.1’s circle, and the circle Lesson 9.2 will fill, need that all-directions length. Production therefore stores length(p) = 0.50, not 0.70. Walk-around stays in the HTML lab so you can see the diamond you would paint if a later shader added the two members. The iPhone 16 Pro explorer does not store it. Lesson 9.2 will reuse that diamond as the wrong silhouette for a circle.

      

    

    
The 0.25 row is not a length. 0.09 + 0.16 is the area of the square built on the long side. If you stored 0.25 as gray you would be storing that area, not the side 0.50. The square root is the step that turns the area back into the side. Lesson 9.2 then subtracts a radius from 0.50. It does not subtract from 0.70 or from 0.25.

8 · iOS ROUTE
One length, one gray store, a square panel

    

      
[[ stitchable ]] half4 distanceGray(
    float2 position,
    half4 color,
    float2 resolution,
    float scale
) {
    float2 fragCoord = position * scale;
    float2 uv = fragCoord / resolution;
    float2 p = uv - float2(0.5, 0.5);
    float d = length(p);
    return half4(d, d, d, 1.0);
}

private let panelSizePt: CGFloat = 360
private let teachPx: Float = 0.30
private let teachPy: Float = -0.40

ShaderRect(
    shader: ShaderLibrary.distanceGray(
        .float2(pixelWidth, pixelHeight),
        .float(Float(displayScale))
    )
)
.frame(width: panelSizePt, height: panelSizePt)
// SwiftUI Canvas overlay: center cross, teaching mark, triangle

      

        
What each part changes

        
panelSizePt = 360 is a square teaching size in points so UV x and UV y use the same pixel length. It is not glass geometry.

        
Xcode compiles distanceGray in the .metal file. ShaderLibrary looks it up. position is points, so fragCoord = position * scale and resolution are both in pixels. scale is displayScale. pixelWidth and pixelHeight are 360 × displayScale. There is no runtime string compiler. Pass both arguments. Leaving one out still compiles the Swift file, and SwiftUI refuses that draw.

11 · EXERCISES
Answer before opening each hidden solution

    
On the countable triangle, why is the long side 5 rather than 3 + 4 = 7?
3 + 4 is the walk along the two legs, through the right-angle point. The long side is the one remaining segment. It never visits that right-angle point. The area rule is 3 × 3 + 4 × 4 = 9 + 16 = 25, and 5 × 5 = 25, so the long side is 5.

    
Diagnostic: at p = (0.30, −0.40), someone stores gray 0.70. Which path did they measure, and what does production store?
They measured walk-around: abs(0.30) + abs(−0.40) = 0.70. Production measures the long side: sqrt(0.09 + 0.16) = 0.50. The equal-value line for 0.70 walk-around is a diamond whose corners go to white. The equal-value line for 0.50 length is a circle, and the square’s corners stay at 0.707 gray.

    
In Straight-line gray, why is the region outside the green circle still dark instead of white?
The green circle is every pixel that stores the selected length, 0.50 at the teaching pixel. It is not a cutoff after which the store becomes 1.00. This square’s farthest corner only has length sqrt(0.25 + 0.25) = 0.707, so those pixels are still closer to black than to white. White would require storing 1.00. Walk-around gray does that at the corners, which is why that picture looks lighter there.

    
In Walk-around gray, what is the analog of a smaller-radius circle, and why does that outline look like a square rotated 45 degrees?
The analog is a smaller walk-around number. Pixels with abs(p.x) + abs(p.y) = 0.25 sit on a smaller diamond and store gray 0.25, the same role the length-0.25 circle plays in Straight-line gray. That diamond is a square standing on a vertex because equal walk-around 0.50 joins the four edge-middles with four equal sides that meet at square corners. The vertices point along the axes, not at the teaching square’s corners. A corner needs walk-around 1.00, so the 0.50 diamond does not reach it.

    
p.y is −0.40. Why is the stored distance 0.50, not −0.50?
The vertical square uses (−0.40) × (−0.40) = 0.16. The square root used here is the one that is zero or greater, because a length is not negative. The minus on p.y only named the top half. It does not become a minus on d. Lesson 9.2 will create a useful minus by subtracting a radius.

    
Why is this lesson’s teaching rectangle square, when Lesson 8.11’s band rectangle was 400 × 160?
Lesson 8.11 only read uv.x, so height did not enter the number. This lesson adds p.x × p.x and p.y × p.y. Those two UV members are the same kind of length only when width equals height. A 400 × 160 rectangle would stretch the “circle” of equal d into an ellipse in pixel space.
