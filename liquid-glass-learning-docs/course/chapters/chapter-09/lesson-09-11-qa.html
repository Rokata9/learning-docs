The objects on this page

  • Wallpaper: the input picture, here a grid of dark lines. Output: the picture that the shader paints. A pixel is one dot of the output.
  • C = (0.50, 0.50): the center of the circle. d: the distance from C to a pixel. sd = d − 0.25: the signed distance, below 0 inside the circle, 0 on the circle edge, above 0 outside.
  • Push: the move from a pixel to its read point, the place of the wallpaper whose color the pixel takes. The push points toward C. Its length is push = dispLen × falloff = 0.10 × falloff. Falloff is a number from 0 to 1: the part of the full push that the pixel gets.
  • The white line x = 0.65: a straight vertical line on the wallpaper, drawn only for tracking. In an output picture a thin white line marks where the white line is in the wallpaper.
  • The light-green curve: the output pixels whose read point lies on the white line. These pixels show the white line.
  • Inner ring (d = 0.15), circle edge (d = 0.25), outer ring (d = 0.35). The band is the space between the inner ring and the outer ring.
  • W = (0.65, 0.30): the white line crosses the circle edge here. P = (0.65, 0.183772): the white line crosses the outer ring here.

Three rules for falloff appear on this page: hard if (the lesson's old button “on/off”, see card 1), linear, and smoothstep. The lesson values are radius 0.25, band 0.10, and dispLen 0.10.

Numbers. All numbers are rounded to 6 decimals: a 5 in the 7th decimal rounds up. Every line of a calculation does one operation and uses the numbers of the lines above it, so a calculator gives the same result. Squares that feed a square root are written out in full.

Points. Click any drawn point to see its name, its role, and its coordinates. With a keyboard: Tab, then Enter or Space. Close with Escape, the ✕ button, or a click outside.

Question 1

What's this on/off option, it's completely unknown to me what it's doing?

What does the “on/off” button do?

“On/off” is the third rule for falloff. Every pixel inside the circle gets the full push, 0.10. Every pixel on the circle edge or outside it gets no push. There is no band where the push gets shorter little by little. Lesson 9.3 used the same rule for colors and called it the hard if. So the lesson and this page now call it “hard if”.

Falloff rule
  • Full color: push = 0.10 (the full push). White: push = 0 (no push).
  • Dark circles: the inner ring (d = 0.15), the circle edge (d = 0.25, thicker), the outer ring (d = 0.35).
  • Sample pixels, named by their distance to C: d = 0.12, 0.20, 0.24, 0.26, 0.30, 0.40.
  • Push arrow: from the pixel to its read point.

The same push lengths along one ray from C. The horizontal axis is d, the distance from C. The bold line is the chosen rule; the two pale lines are the other two rules. The six points are the six sample pixels.

The rule, as code

falloff = (sd < 0.0) ? 1.0 : 0.0; hard if push = dispLen × falloff = 0.10 × falloff

Read it as: if sd is below 0 (the pixel is inside the circle), falloff is 1; otherwise falloff is 0. So there are only two cases:

inside the circle (sd below 0): falloff = 1 → push = 0.10 × 1 = 0.10 on the edge or outside (sd ≥ 0): falloff = 0 → push = 0.10 × 0 = 0

What we see. With “hard if” the map is one evenly colored disc with a sharp edge exactly on the circle edge. With “linear” or smoothstep, the disc is evenly colored only inside the inner ring. Across the band it fades to white, and it is white from the outer ring on.

Why the lesson shows this rule at all. It is the simplest possible rule. The lesson uses it only to show what goes wrong without a band: the break (card 6).

t tells how far sd has moved through the band, as a fraction from 0 to 1. At sd = +0.05, sd has moved 0.15 into a band that is 0.20 wide, so t = 0.15 ÷ 0.20 = 0.75. It was wrong, and the box in the lesson now writes every operation. Lesson 9.3 called the same number linearFraction. It used the same example: sd = +0.05 gives 0.75.

What are 0.249 and 0.251, and what are the “two sides” of W?

Yes, W is exactly at d = 0.25. The numbers 0.249 and 0.251 are not W. They belong to two other pixels next to W, on the ray from C through W. Pixel I is 0.001 closer to C, so it is just inside the circle. Pixel O is 0.001 farther from C, so it is just outside. “Both sides of W” means the inside and the outside of the circle edge. The box compared the push of I with the push of O. The goal was to show that the push does not jump when a pixel crosses the circle edge. The lesson never drew or named I and O.

Why the difference matters. I and O are neighbours: only 2 × h apart. If their pushes differ a lot, their read points are far apart. Then no pixel reads the wallpaper between those two read points. Question 5

Is the “on/off” button broken?

No. The button never switched the effect off. It selects the hard-if rule, and the hard-if rule gives every pixel inside the circle the full push. So the inside of the circle stays changed. Only the outside of the circle shows the plain wallpaper again. The name “on/off” made the button look like an off switch. This is why the lesson now calls it “hard if” and says that it is not an off switch.

The very middle looks turned around — why?

Closer than 0.15 to C, all three rules give the full push 0.10. (Hard if: sd is below 0. Linear and smoothstep: sd is below −0.10, so t is limited to 0 and falloff = 1.) So the middle looks the same with all three rules.

Closer than 0.10 to C, the push (0.10) is longer than the distance to C. The read point then goes past C, to the other side. So the wallpaper around C appears turned inside out. This makes the flower shape in the middle. The picture below shows two such pixels, Z and Z2.

Where the lesson failed. Section 2, “See the difference”: the button label on/off reads as “switch the effect on or off”. The button really picks a falloff rule. The lesson also never explained why the very middle looks turned around.

Part 2 — why it is a curve: equal steps down, unequal steps sideways

Walk down the light-green curve from y = 0.25 to its peak at y = 0.50, in 5 equal steps of 0.05. A straight line moves right by the same amount at every equal step. The light-green curve moves right by 0.013432, 0.0145, 0.012919, 0.008755, and 0.003071. These are five different amounts. So no two neighbouring pieces lie on one straight line: the line turns at every step. The gray line from P to the peak moves 0.010541 at every step, because it is straight.

Question 9

also state if the linear formula is still a curve what formula it uses compared to if it were straight line

With “linear”, which formula draws the light-green curve — and what would a straight line use?

Two different formulas are at work. The first is the falloff formula. With “linear” it is a straight line in the falloff graph: falloff = (0.35 − d) ÷ 0.20 inside the band. The second gives the position of the light-green curve in the picture: x = 0.60 + 0.0175 ÷ r. Here r is the distance from C to the point of the white line that the pixel shows. A straight line in the picture needs the form x = (a fixed number) + (a fixed number) × y. The gray line has this form: x = 0.611257 + 0.210819 × y. The curve's formula divides by r, and r contains a square root, so it does not have this form.

    Where the lesson failed. Section 2, “Linear does not mean a straight line”: the lesson said only “Linear” makes only the graph of falloff against sd straight, not the picture. It gave no formula for the curve in the picture, so the claim could not be checked.
    Changed in the lesson. The formula x = 0.60 + 0.0175 ÷ r is in the text, next to the gray line's formula, with its derivation in an expandable box.

    Question 10

    at y = 0.40 the curve is at x = 0.7048 - explain also by what formula is this calculated in the linear case....

    How is x = 0.7048 at y = 0.40 calculated with “linear”?

    No short formula turns y = 0.40 straight into x. The number comes from one condition: the pixel (x, 0.40) must read the white line, so its read point must have x = 0.65. With “linear”, this condition is one equation in x. The equation has x inside a square root and also outside it, so no short solving formula exists. The page therefore tries values of x until the read point lands on 0.65. The result is x = 0.704841.

    Step A — the condition, written as an equation

    Every line on the left is one step of the lesson's chain, written for a pixel (x, 0.40) with an unknown x. The last line is the condition. Squaring away the root turns it into an equation with x⁴. Such an equation has no short formula for x.

    The push formula push = 0.175 − 0.5 × d is linear falloff written with d. Card 9, steps 1 and 2, derives it.

    Step B — solving by trying, one decimal at a time

    • A trial pixel whose read point is left of the white line (readUv.x below 0.65).
    • A trial pixel whose read point is right of the white line (above 0.65).
    • A trial pixel that reads exactly 0.65 (6 decimals).
    • readUv.x for every x on the row (linear).

    What the page does: the same search, but it halves the range 60 times instead of going one decimal at a time. After 60 halvings the range is far below one millionth, so both ways give 0.704841.

    Step C — a second way, with the exact formula from card 9

    With “linear”, the formula x = 0.60 + 0.0175 ÷ r gives the same x = 0.704841. But this way needs R_K.y = 0.426772 first. That number is also unknown until the equation of Step A is solved. So for a given row, solving is always needed. The formula is exact once the point of the white line is chosen.

    Where the lesson failed. Section 2, “Linear does not mean a straight line”: at y = 0.40 the curve is at x = 0.7048, and its ⓘ said The page finds this pixel by searching the row y = 0.40 for the pixel that reads x = 0.65. It named a search but showed neither the equation nor the search.
    Changed in the lesson. The text writes the condition as an equation, says why it has no short solution, lists the trial values, and gives the formula x = 0.60 + 0.0175 ÷ r.
