Lesson 9.11 — Refraction as displaced sampling

How the glass circle bends the picture behind it. Light theme · desktop · runs from file://.

A pixel is the smallest colored dot of the picture. Hold a clear marble over a striped tablecloth: the stripes under the marble bend. That bend is called refraction — light changing direction when it crosses into a new material. This lesson builds that bend with one idea: every pixel reads the wallpaper a little away from itself, and the circle says how far. The wallpaper is the input picture the shader reads colors from — here, the orange/navy checker from Lesson 9.7. A shader is the small program that runs once for every pixel.

The whole lesson is one chain of small steps, run once per pixel:

p = uv − C how far the pixel sits from the circle center d = length(p) that distance as one number sd = d − radius how far past the circle edge (signed) falloff = 1 − smoothstep(−band, band, sd) how much of the full push this pixel gets dir = p / d which way is "away from the center" disp = dir × dispLen × falloff the push arrow readUv = uv − disp where the pixel reads the wallpaper

Sections 1 and 3 explain every line of this chain in pictures and then in full numbers. Section 2 defends the one curve in it: why smoothstep and not a straight line. Everything after that is playing with it.

Section 1 · the idea in picturesThe problem: one push for everyone moves too much

Lesson 9.10 already taught the read to move: disp = dir × dispLen, then readUv = uv − disp. Every pixel moved its read by the same length, dispLen = 0.10. Look at what that does to the far line:

The fix is one new multiplication: disp = dir × dispLen × falloff. falloff is a number from 0 to 1 that says how much of the full push one pixel gets: 1 means the full push, 0 means no push, 0.5 means half the push. We multiply by falloff because pixels far from the circle must not move at all, and pixels near the circle should move smoothly less and less — a hard on/off switch would break the picture at the circle edge (Section 2 shows it).

Where does falloff come from? From the signed distance sd, through the smoothstep curve from Lesson 9.3:

falloff = 1 − smoothstep(−band, band, sd) with band = 0.10 sd = −0.10 (deep enough inside) → smoothstep = 0 → falloff = 1 full push sd = 0 (on the circle edge) → smoothstep = 0.5 → falloff = 0.5 half push sd = +0.10 (far enough outside) → smoothstep = 1 → falloff = 0 no push

Same curve, new job. Lesson 9.3 used this exact smoothstep curve as a color mixer called mask: it blended navy and orange to soften an edge. Here falloff is a length scale: it stretches or shrinks the push arrow. It does not paint a soft edge, and it does not cut a hole in the picture. It only decides how long each pixel's push arrow is.

Section 2 · why the curveWhy smoothstep and not a straight line?

The obvious simpler choice than smoothstep is a straight line. Call it the linear falloff: falloff = 1 − lin(−band, band, sd), where lin(a, b, x) = (x − a) / (b − a), clamped to stay between 0 and 1. At three key points it gives the same values as smoothstep: 1 at sd = −0.10, 0.5 at sd = 0, and 0 at sd = +0.10. The two circles where the band starts and ends are the band rings: the inner band ring (sd = −0.10, distance 0.15 from the circle center C = (0.50, 0.50)) and the outer band ring (sd = +0.10, distance 0.35 from C). The buttons below also offer the most basic choice, the on/off falloff: 1 inside the circle, 0 outside, nothing in between.

See it: one chain, three falloffs

What to look for. linear: the grid lines change direction sharply where they cross a band ring. At P the lime curve changes direction at once, by the angle in the readout. That is a corner. smoothstep: the same lines bend gradually, and at P the lime curve does not change direction. on/off: press at the circle edge — the lines break apart at the circle. Press Show the band rings: every corner sits exactly on a band ring. On the photo the corners are hard to see. The corners show on straight lines: text lines, list rows, icon edges. The grid wallpaper exists only on this page; on the iPhone the wallpaper is checkerBackground.

"Linear" describes the falloff graph, not the picture. With the linear falloff the lime line is still not straight. At y = 0.40 the linear lime curve sits at x = 0.7048. The straight join from P to the peak of the linear lime curve, (0.7167, 0.50), passes x = 0.65 + (0.40 − 0.1838) ÷ (0.50 − 0.1838) × 0.0667 = 0.6956 at that height. The reason is the one from Lesson 9.10: the distance d changes along the line like a square root, and the push direction turns. The linear and smoothstep lime curves stay within 0.005 of each other. The linear and smoothstep lime curves differ only in how they leave the band rings.

Why at the band rings? The reads along one ray

A ray is a straight path that starts at C and goes outward. Take the ray along the row y = 0.50, to the right of C. Press Next; the falloff buttons work at every step.

Why a squeeze that switches on at once makes a corner

On the outer band ring every pixel has falloff 0 and reads itself, so nothing is squeezed along the ring. So near P the squeeze from step 5 of the ray figure acts only along the ray. A line that crosses the ring at an angle moves partly along the ray and partly along the ring. Split one short step of the input line at P into a part along the ray and a part along the ring, squeeze only the ray part, and the step turns.

Is smoothstep common practice?

Yes. Shaders turn a distance into a soft 0-to-1 value all the time — soft edges (Lesson 9.3), glows, shadows, and falloffs like this one — so Metal, GLSL (OpenGL), and HLSL (Direct3D) all ship smoothstep as a built-in function. What makes it special: 3t² − 2t³ is the simplest polynomial that goes from 0 to 1 and starts and ends flat, so its drop per step starts near 0 and ends near 0. That is the ease-in-out from Chapter 5, applied to distance instead of time. It costs three more arithmetic operations per pixel than lin — count the three operations in t × t × (3 − 2t) — while the chain already runs a square root and a division per pixel. That cost is an estimate from counting, not a measurement on the iPhone.

What this page cannot tell you. Apple's own Liquid Glass shader is not public, so this lesson does not claim which curve Apple uses. Knowing it would need Apple's source code or documentation. The unknown does not change the choice for our chain: a curve that starts and ends flat avoids the corners either way.

Browser vs iPhone. The iPhone chain in chapter09/RefractionSampling.metal keeps Metal's built-in smoothstep. The on/off and linear buttons exist only on this page. If the Metal file used lin instead, the iPhone picture would get the same corners on the band rings: the chain is identical, only the runner differs.

Why bother moving the read? Because the move changes colors

W happened to read the same navy it sat on. Pixel A = (0.76, 0.50) is luckier for us: without the move A sits in a navy cell, but its read crosses the cell edge x = 0.75 into an orange cell — so A stores orange instead of navy.

Section 5 · the lengths, measuredHow long are the push arrows along the curve?

The lime curve is the apparent line of x = 0.65: at each height y, the one output pixel whose read lands exactly on x = 0.65 in the input. Now measure the push arrows along the curve — the arrows from each pixel on the curve to its read position on the input line. Question to answer: at the peak, the center height y = 0.50, is the arrow the full 0.10 long?

The three arrows on the curve

arrow length = |disp| = dispLen × falloff (dir has length 1, so it never changes the length) P1 = (0.721083, 0.50) — the peak: |disp| = 0.10 × 0.710831 = 0.071083 reads (0.65, 0.50) P2 = (0.707927, 0.40): |disp| = 0.10 × 0.642790 = 0.064279 reads (0.65, 0.427859) P3 = (0.675169, 0.30): |disp| = 0.10 × 0.382010 = 0.038201 reads (0.65, 0.328737) bend ends (y = 0.1838, y = 0.8162): |disp| = 0.10 × 0 = 0

No — at the peak the arrow is 0.071083 long, not 0.10. P1 sits at d = 0.221083 from the center, inside the band, so its falloff is 0.710831 and its arrow gets about 71% of the full length. The full 0.10 lives deeper inside the circle, where falloff = 1 (sd ≤ −0.10): inside the inner band ring every arrow is exactly 0.10 long. The amber arrow shows one: the pixel (0.65, 0.50), sitting exactly on the input line at the center height, has sd = −0.10 and reads (0.55, 0.50).

Watch where the arrows aim: left, and a little toward the center height — the read is pulled back toward the center. P2 at height 0.40 reads at height 0.4279; P3 at height 0.30 reads at height 0.3287.

Why it is a curve — and why this section does not re-prove it

Lesson 9.10 already proved it for the same construction: the push follows d = sqrt((u − 0.50)² + (y − 0.50)²) and the smoothstep curve, and neither is linear on either side of the center height; three read positions on one side of the center did not lie on one straight line. The same argument holds here — falloff adds one more curved ingredient — so this lesson reuses that result and spends this section on the lengths instead. And the curve never breaks: the input line is one connected line, every chain step changes smoothly with height, so neighboring heights read neighboring input spots and points keep their order.

One more curve in the same picture

The checker edge x = 0.75 also gets an apparent curve — the violet curve in the lab. It is not the lime curve's twin: its pixels sit farther from the center, so their sd is bigger, their falloff is smaller, and the violet curve's peak push is only 0.0289, against the lime curve's 0.0711. Same mechanism, different sd. The lab's peaks line shows both numbers live.

Section 6 · short code excerptThe shader on the iPhone

This is the real Metal function from the lab, chapter09/RefractionSampling.metal (explorer: chapter09/RefractionSamplingExplorer.swift). Metal runs it once per pixel of the SwiftUI view. The six middle lines are the chain from Section 3, word for word.

[[ stitchable ]] half4 refractSample(
    float2 position, half4 color,
    float2 resolution, float dispLen, float radius, float band, float scale
) {
    float2 fragCoord = position * scale;        // points → pixels
    float2 uv = fragCoord / resolution;         // pixels → UV (0..1 across, 0..1 down)
    float2 p = uv - float2(0.5, 0.5);           // step 1: position from center
    float d = length(p);                        // step 2: distance
    float2 disp = float2(0.0, 0.0);
    if (d > 0.0001) {                           // guard: never divide by d = 0
        float sd = d - radius;                  // step 3: signed distance
        float falloff = 1.0 - smoothstep(-band, band, sd);   // step 4: how much push
        disp = (p / d) * dispLen * falloff;     // steps 5–6: direction × length × falloff
    }
    float2 readUv = uv - disp;                  // step 7: the read position
    return checkerBackground(readUv * resolution, resolution);  // step 8: the color
}
  • checkerBackground is a plain Metal function in the same file — the same orange/navy 4×4 checker as Lesson 9.10. On iOS, reading the wallpaper is a function call, not a texture sample (Lessons 9.7–9.9).
  • The guard if (d > 0.0001) skips the divide at the exact center, because p / 0 has no answer. Why the cutoff is 0.0001 and not exactly 0: the Metal source does not say, and this lesson has not demonstrated a need for that wider cutoff. What we know for sure: at d = 0 the division is undefined, so some guard must exist.
  • At the exact center (d ≤ 0.0001), disp stays (0, 0): the center pixel reads itself. That is honest — "away from the center" has no direction at the center.
Browser vs iPhone. In this page, JavaScript runs the same chain once per canvas pixel and paints a <canvas>. On the iPhone, Metal runs refractSample once per pixel of the layer on the GPU. The math and the wallpaper are identical; only the runner differs. What the framework owns: how many pixels the layer has, when it calls the shader, and how it schedules the GPU — none of that is visible from this lesson, and none of it changes the math. What we see and check here is exactly the part the shader computes.

Section 7 · coding challengeChallenge: place the apparent middle by hand

With the lesson values, the apparent middle of the line x = 0.65 lands at about x = 0.721 at the center height. Your job: choose dispLen so the apparent middle at height y = 0.50 lands exactly at x = 0.70. Keep radius = 0.25 and band = 0.10.

The output pixel in question is (0.70, 0.50). Its fixed values — they do not contain dispLen: p = (0.20, 0), d = 0.20, sd = 0.20 − 0.25 = −0.05, falloff = 1 − smoothstep(−0.10, 0.10, −0.05) = 0.84375, dir = (1, 0). You need readUv.x = 0.70 − 1 × dispLen × 0.84375 = 0.65.
