Chapter 9 · Why the squares tilt

Build a displacement field

Hard question: The input cells are axis-aligned squares. The store shows those same cells leaning, like a checker painted on a bump. Nobody rotated a square. How does one walk along each ray from C tilt a vertical side, and what else does that walk do?

A vertical side has two ends. Those ends sit on two different rays from C. Each end is painted farther out on its own ray, same walk length. They no longer share an x. That is the tilt. The rest of the bump is the same walk, on every side of every cell.

Canonical wording: displacement, unit pair, eval.

1 · THE MACHINE, ONCE

Every input point is painted farther from C on its own ray.

Lesson 9.8 already split write and read: this pixel stores; eval asks a possibly different pixel. This lesson’s ask is not a shared slide. At a store pixel W, walk a fixed UV length dispLen toward C and copy that neighbor. The inverse picture is the one that explains the squares: an input point Q is seen in the store at the point you reach by walking that same length away from C, along the ray through Q.

What we will not re-teach

uv from the top-left. p = uv − 0.5 names the same pixel from C. length(p) is how far that is. eval wants pixels. Write does not move.

The new walk

Same length at every pixel except C. Direction is the ray from C through this pixel. Default length 0.10, one tenth of the square.

Why divide exists, in one line

p already points along the ray, but its length is “how far from C,” different at every pixel. Divide by that length so the walk we put back on is the length we chose, not the leftover distance.

3 · THE SAME WALK ON FOUR CORNERS

A square becomes a trapezoid that leans toward C.

Step 5 of the construction above does this to one pink square. Each corner has its own ray. Each corner is painted farther out on that ray, same walk. Connect the four new corners. The vertical sides tilt for the reason in section 2. The horizontal sides tilt for the same reason with x and y swapped: two ends that shared a y sit on two rays, so the walk splits differently, and they no longer share a y.

The side closer to C is the short one. The side farther from C is the long one. Sides lean as if they were vanishing toward C. That is the “tilted squares” in the stored checker. Every checker cell is four sides. Every side that is not already a ray through C does this.

A side that already lies on a ray through C does not tilt. Both ends share that one ray, so they stay on the same line. The vertical through C stays vertical. The horizontal through C stays horizontal. The stored picture keeps that cross. Everything off the cross leans.
4 · THE REST OF THE BUMP

Bow, bigger middle, still a flat square.

The two-end picture is a straight slant. The real vertical line has a point at every y, each on its own ray. Step 5 draws that as a green curve. The stored edge is not only tilted. It is bowed. That is why cell edges look bent, not just diamond-cut.

Bow

Every point on a vertical line has a different leftover p, so a different split of the walk. The image of the line is a curve. Horizontal lines bow for the same reason.

Bigger middle

Near C the leftover walk is short, so the same 0.10 is a large fraction of it. Those cells stretch more. Far from C, 0.10 is a small nudge. The middle looks thicker. Inside the dashed disk, the leftover is smaller than 0.10, so the ask walks past C.

Not zoom, not 3D

Zoom would move a far corner farther than a near corner by a growing offset. This walk is the same length on every ray, which is why a vertical side tilts instead of staying vertical and merely scaling. The draw rectangle is still flat. The “3D” is a checker whose sides lean toward C and whose middle is stretched.

6 · ANDROID ROUTE
The AGSL is the inward ask that paints the outward square.

    
The construction walked input points out. The shader, at each store pixel, walks in and copies. Those are inverses. Subtract is the inward walk. Divide by length is how a diagonal ray still gets a walk of 0.10 instead of the leftover distance.

    

      
uniform shader background;
uniform float2 resolution;
uniform float dispLen;

half4 main(float2 fragCoord) {
    float2 uv = fragCoord / resolution;
    float2 p = uv - float2(0.5, 0.5);
    float d = length(p);
    float2 disp = float2(0.0, 0.0);
    if (d > 0.0001) {
        disp = (p / d) * dispLen;
    }
    float2 readUv = uv - disp;
    return background.eval(readUv * resolution);
}

      

        
p / dLength-1 direction along this pixel’s ray. At H that is (1, 0). At Q that is about (0.71, −0.71), the 45° split.

        
* dispLenPuts the chosen walk on that direction. Same 0.10 at H and at Q. That equal length is why Q cannot keep up in x, which is the tilt.

        
uv - dispInward ask. The store pixel copies the neighbor toward C. That is how an input point appears farther out.

        
eval(readUv * resolution)Lesson 9.7 leftover. eval wants pixels.

9 · EXERCISES
Answer before opening each hidden solution

    

      H is at (0.75, 0.50) and walks to (0.85, 0.50). Q is at (0.75, 0.25). Why is Q′ not at x = 0.85 as well?
      
Q is 0.25 right of C and 0.25 up from C. Its ray is the 45° diagonal, so a walk of 0.10 splits: about 0.07 right and 0.07 up. H’s ray is horizontal, so H uses the whole 0.10 going right. Same walk length, different split, different new x. That is the tilt.

    

    

      The vertical line through C, x = 0.50, does not look tilted in the store. Why not?
      
Every point on that line already sits on the same ray (straight up or straight down from C). Both ends of a side on that line stay on that line after the walk. A side tilts only when its two ends sit on two different rays.

    

    

      Why do cells near C look more warped than cells near the edge?
      
The walk is the same length 0.10 everywhere. Near C that 0.10 is a large fraction of the leftover distance, so a small input cell is painted across a much larger store region. Far from C, 0.10 is a small nudge on a long leftover. The two ends of a near-C side also have rays that spread apart more quickly, so the tilt is stronger.

    

    

      If the shader used p as the arrow instead of (p / length(p)) × dispLen, would a vertical side still tilt?
      
No. Then each point would walk a distance equal to its leftover length (or a fixed fraction of it). That is Lesson 9.9’s growing offset: a zoom. H at leftover 0.25 and Q at leftover 0.35 would walk different lengths along similar-enough rays that the side stays a scaled vertical, not a slant from a split walk of equal length.

11 · EVIDENCE BOUNDARY
What this lesson establishes

    

      
Established
The tilted squares are one walk, on two rays. A vertical side’s ends no longer share an x because the horizontal end spends the whole walk going right and the diagonal end splits it. Four corners do that → trapezoid leaning toward C. The rest is bow (every point has its own ray) and a bigger middle (same walk is a larger fraction near C). The shader’s inward ask is the inverse of that outward paint. Default dispLen = 0.10. C copies C.
