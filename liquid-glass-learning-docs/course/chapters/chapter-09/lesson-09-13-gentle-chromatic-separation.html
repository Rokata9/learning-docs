Lesson 9.13 — Gentle chromatic separation

Read the wallpaper three times: once for red, once for green, once for blue. Light theme · desktop · works from file://.

In Lesson 9.11 every pixel W had one read point: the point of the wallpaper where W takes its color. W copied all three channels of that color — red, green, and blue — from that one point. Real glass bends blue light a little more than red light. So in this lesson every pixel gets three read points, very close together: one for its red channel, one for green, one for blue. This is chromatic separation (“chromatic” means “about color”): the three channels are read at slightly different places. Along the edges of the picture, it paints thin colored strips: the fringe.

  • The wallpaper: the checkerboard of Lesson 9.7, in black and white. White is (1, 1, 1): all three channels at 1. Black is (0, 0, 0): all three at 0.
  • The violet circle: the glass from Lesson 9.11, radius 0.25. Its center (the violet dot) is C = (0.40, 0.54).
  • The fringe: colored strips. They appear where the three read points of one pixel land on different colors.

Why black and white: in this wallpaper the red, green and blue channels are the same picture. So every color you see in the right picture comes from the three read points, not from the wallpaper.

Section 2 · the new linesThree read points in the code

Lesson 9.12 had one read point, readUv. Three lines replace it, and one new line builds the color:

readRed = clamp(uv − disp × (1 − spread), 0, 1) new: a push 10% shorter (spread = 0.10) readGreen = clamp(uv − disp, 0, 1) the Lesson 9.12 read point, unchanged readBlue = clamp(uv − disp × (1 + spread), 0, 1) new: a push 10% longer color = half4(colRed.r, colGreen.g, colBlue.b, 1.0) new: one channel from each read point
  • disp is the push arrow of Lesson 9.11: disp = dir × push. disp × 0.90 is the same arrow, 0.90 times as long. The direction does not change, so the three read points always lie on one line from W toward C.
  • spread is the one new number. It says how much shorter the red push is, and how much longer the blue push is, than the green push.
  • clamp is the Lesson 9.12 line. Each read point gets its own clamp, so none of the three can leave the wallpaper.
  • colRed is the wallpaper's color at readRed: a half4 with four members, r, g, b, a. colRed.r is its red member. The last line takes the red member of colRed, the green member of colGreen, and the blue member of colBlue. Alpha is 1.0 because every wallpaper color is opaque.

Why blue gets the longest push

Real glass bends blue light a little more than red light. A glass prism shows this: white light goes in, and its colors come out at slightly different angles, so the white fans out into a rainbow. The physics name for this is dispersion. In photos and on screens, the colored edges that dispersion causes are called chromatic aberration.

The shader copies the order: the blue channel gets the longest push, the red channel the shortest, and green is in between. Because a longer push is a stronger bend, blue is bent the most, as in the prism.

Apple does not document whether its own Liquid Glass separates colors, or by how much, and its shader is not public. Only Apple's documentation or source code could tell us. So the course keeps the effect small — gentle — and lets you judge it by eye below.

Which color a strip gets, and how strong it looks

A channel is a number from 0 (none of that light) to 1 (all of it). A bar below shows one channel: an empty bar is 0, a full bar is 1. Each row follows the line x = 0.40 down through the edge y = 0.50, as in the picture above (spread 0.40, so that the strips are wide enough to see; at 0.10 the strips have the same colors and are 0.01 wide).

  • What “a jump of 1” means. From white to black every bar goes from full to empty: each channel changes by 1 − 0 = 1, the whole bar. Dark text on a white row (bottom row) is almost the same: 1 − 0.067 = 0.933 in every channel.
  • Which color a strip gets. Strip 1 lies between ③ and ②: only readBlue has crossed the edge, so strip 1 is the upper color with the blue channel of the lower color. Strip 2 lies between ② and ①: only readRed has not crossed, so strip 2 is the lower color with the red channel of the upper color. Each strip differs from its neighbor in one channel (the framed bar).
  • How strong it looks is the size of that one change. Black and white: 1 and 1, two full bars, so pure yellow and pure red. Orange and navy: strip 1 changes blue by only 0.22, a quarter of a bar, so it is an orange that is slightly pinker and hard to see. Strip 2 changes red by 0.78, almost a full bar, so it is a strong raspberry red. On orange and navy one strip almost vanishes and the other stays clear.

Why the words under the glass stay words

With screen in the lab, the words under the glass are wider, thinner, or mirrored, but each word stays in one piece. The pictures below show why. They use one read point per pixel (spread 0), so only the bend is visible. Nine pixels, 0.02 apart, sit around the pixel G. Their read points are drawn on the wallpaper.

Place of G
Output: the 9 pixels, 0.04 from first to last
Wallpaper: where the 9 pixels read
  • the lime grid: the 9 pixels (left) and their 9 read points (right), joined in the same order. T, B, L and R are the top, bottom, left and right pixel and their read points.
  • the violet square (right): the place of the 9 pixels, drawn on the wallpaper
  • the pink push arrow: from G's place to G's read point
  • C and the violet circle: the glass, as in the lab (“this lesson”)
  • Neighbors stay neighbors. The push changes smoothly from place to place (the graph has no jump). So two neighboring pixels have neighboring read points, in the same order: at all four places, the lime lines on the wallpaper never cross. A letter is a group of neighboring dark pixels. So each letter is read in one piece, and it never mixes with another letter.
  • What sets the size: the push curve (the graph). The push depends only on the distance from C. At these four places the push points up or down (on the glass edge, almost), so L to R runs across the push and T to B along it. Across: the read points sit closer to C than the pixels, so they are closer together, and the letters look wider. Along: where T and B get the same push (inside), the gap stays 0.04. On the glass edge, T is closer to C and gets the longer push, so the gap grows, and the letters look thinner. Outside the orange ring the push is 0, and nothing changes.
  • Middle: closer to C than 0.10, the push (0.10) is longer than the distance to C, so the read points pass C. L and R swap sides, so the letters are mirrored. Exactly 0.10 from C, every pixel reads C itself, so close to that ring the letters do come apart: the petal shapes in the middle of the lab picture.
  • Where the curve comes from. It is the rule chosen in Lesson 9.11: full push near C, smoothstep across the band, no push outside. It is not computed from the shape of a real piece of glass. Chapter 10 describes the glass by its height, and Lesson 10.11 lets the thickness change the bend.

Section 5 · short code excerptThe iPhone shader

The Metal function for the LiquidGlassLab app. It belongs in chapter09/ChromaticSeparation.metal, with the explorer screen chapter09/ChromaticSeparationExplorer.swift. The parameter list is packed onto fewer lines here, and the comments on the right were added for the lesson. The new lines are green.

[[ stitchable ]] half4 chromaticRefract(
    float2 position, half4 color, float2 resolution, float2 center,
    float dispLen, float radius, float band, float spread, float scale
) {
    float2 fragCoord = position * scale;
    float2 uv = fragCoord / resolution;
    float2 p = uv - center;
    float d = length(p);
    float2 disp = float2(0.0, 0.0);
    if (d > 0.0001) {
        float sd = d - radius;
        float falloff = 1.0 - smoothstep(-band, band, sd);
        disp = (p / d) * dispLen * falloff;
    }
    float2 readRed   = clamp(uv - disp * (1.0 - spread), 0.0, 1.0);   // new: shorter push
    float2 readGreen = clamp(uv - disp, 0.0, 1.0);                    // the Lesson 9.12 read point
    float2 readBlue  = clamp(uv - disp * (1.0 + spread), 0.0, 1.0);   // new: longer push
    half4 colRed   = blackWhiteChecker(readRed);
    half4 colGreen = blackWhiteChecker(readGreen);
    half4 colBlue  = blackWhiteChecker(readBlue);
    return half4(colRed.r, colGreen.g, colBlue.b, 1.0);               // new: one channel from each
}
  • disp * (1.0 - spread) multiplies both parts of disp by one number, as in Lesson 8.8.
  • blackWhiteChecker is the Lesson 9.7 checkerboard in black and white: col = min(floor(x × 4.0), 3.0), row = min(floor(y × 4.0), 3.0), col + row even gives half4(1.0, 1.0, 1.0, 1.0), odd gives half4(0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 1.0).
  • Only the return line is new code logic. The two new read lines are copies of the green line with one extra multiply.
Browser and iPhone. On this page, JavaScript runs the same chain once for every pixel of the canvas. On iPhone, Metal runs chromaticRefract once for every pixel of the view. The cost: every pixel now asks the wallpaper three times instead of once. Here the wallpaper is a small function (Lesson 9.7). When a later chapter reads the real screen behind the glass, each ask reads the screen's pixels, so three asks cost more GPU time than one. How much more is a measurement for the performance chapter, not a number we know yet.

Section 7 · exercisesExercises

Answer first in your head or on paper. Then open the hidden answer. Every answer draws the question and the reason.

Exercise 1 — the full push, but no fringe?

The pixel W₁ = (0.27, 0.54) is straight left of C, 0.13 away, so it gets the full push 0.10. Does W₁ get a fringe color?

Show the answer

No. W₁ stores black (0, 0, 0), the same as with one read point.

push = 0.10, dir = (−1, 0): dir points left, away from C, so the read points move right, toward C readRed.x = 0.27 − (−0.09) = 0.36 readGreen.x = 0.27 − (−0.10) = 0.37 readBlue.x = 0.27 − (−0.11) = 0.38 all three y = 0.54 readRed col: 0.36 × 4 = 1.44 → floor → 1 readGreen col: 0.37 × 4 = 1.48 → floor → 1 readBlue col: 0.38 × 4 = 1.52 → floor → 1 row: 0.54 × 4 = 2.16 → floor → 2 (all three) col + row = 1 + 2 = 3 → odd → black, for all three

Why: the three read points are only 0.02 apart from first to last. All three land inside the same black cell. The nearest edge, y = 0.50, is 0.04 away. A fringe needs an edge between the read points; the push alone does not make one.

Exercise 2 — red and blue swapped

A classmate gives red the longer push and blue the shorter one: readRed = uv − disp × (1 + spread) and readBlue = uv − disp × (1 − spread). What does W = (0.40, 0.405) store now, and what happens to the two strips?

Show the answer
readBlue = (0.40, 0.405 + 0.09) = (0.40, 0.495) → white → blue = 1 readGreen = (0.40, 0.405 + 0.10) = (0.40, 0.505) → black → green = 0 readRed = (0.40, 0.405 + 0.11) = (0.40, 0.515) → black → red = 0 W stores (0, 0, 1): blue

Why: the read point farthest along the arrow crosses the edge first. Now that is readRed, so the first pixels below white lose red, not blue: (0, 1, 1), cyan. Then they lose green too: (0, 0, 1), blue. The strips keep their places and their width (0.01); only their colors change. Real glass bends blue the most, so the lesson's order is the one that looks like glass.

Exercise 3 — a fixed step instead of a multiply

Another classmate skips the multiply and writes readRed = readGreen + float2(0.01, 0.0) and readBlue = readGreen − float2(0.01, 0.0): always the same small step to the side. What happens far from the glass, outside the orange ring?

Show the answer
far from the glass: push = 0, so disp = (0, 0) and readGreen = W fixed step: readRed = W + (0.01, 0) → a different point from W multiply: disp × 0.90 = (0, 0) × 0.90 = (0, 0) → readRed = W

Why: the lesson's red and blue steps are disp × spread, so they are 0 wherever the push is 0. The fixed step is never 0. So the whole screen gets fringes, also where there is no glass: it looks like a broken screen. The fixed step is also only sideways, so edges that run left to right get no strip at all.
