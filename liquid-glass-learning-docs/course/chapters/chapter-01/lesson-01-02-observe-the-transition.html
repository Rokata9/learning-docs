Chapter 1 · Lesson 1.2

Observe the transition one state at a time

A fast animation hides useful detail. This lesson slows the event down and gives each visible change a clear name.

You will not guess how Apple built the effect. You will first record what the screen actually shows. Those observations will later become acceptance criteria for the Android implementation.

Lesson goal

After this lesson, you should be able to:

  • separate a transition into useful observation stages;
  • compare one sampled state with the previous sampled state;
  • describe boundary, background, content, light, and shadow separately;
  • keep a visible fact separate from an implementation hypothesis.
1 · Three terms

Frame, transition, and stage

New graphics idea: a fast visual change can be inspected as ordered states.
New math idea: none. We only use before and after.

Frame

A frame is one complete image shown by the screen at one moment.

Concrete test: pause a recording. The single paused image is one frame.

Transition

A transition is the sequence of visible changes between one stable state and another stable state.

Concrete test: the control starts small and ends as a menu. Everything between those two states belongs to the transition.

Stage

A stage is a name that we give to a useful group of nearby frames. The name helps us study one purpose, such as press response or content reveal.

Concrete test: several frames can all belong to the “middle expansion” stage.

A stage is an analysis label. The real animation does not need to pause at a stage boundary. A smooth transition can still be divided into stages for study.
1 · RestThe small control is stable.
2 · ContactThe finger reaches the control.
3 · Press responseThe material compresses or brightens.
4 · Early expansionThe boundary starts growing from the source.
5 · Middle expansionThe moving material is neither a button nor a finished sheet.
6 · Content revealMenu rows begin to appear.
7 · Settled sheetThe menu reaches its stable result.
8 · ClosingThe sequence reverses toward the source.

3 · Observation method

Run five separate passes

Do not try to notice everything at once. Rewatch the same transition for one category at a time.

Pass 1 · Boundary

Watch only the outside edge. Record which edges move and which edge stays near the source control.

Pass 2 · Background

Watch one sharp line behind the material. Record whether the line stays sharp, softens, shifts, or bends.

Pass 3 · Content

Watch icons and menu rows. Record when each item first becomes visible and when it becomes stable.

Pass 4 · Light and shadow

Watch bright edges and dark regions. Record whether their position or strength changes.

Pass 5 · Order

Record the order of events. Do not assume two changes start at the same moment unless the frames support that claim.

Real-world pressure test: a recreation can look correct at rest and after settling while looking wrong during expansion. The middle samples therefore need their own acceptance criteria.

7 · Evidence boundary

What this lesson proves and what it does not prove

Observation gives strong acceptance criteria. Observation does not expose private source code.

Supported by visible evidence

You can compare the size and position of the boundary. You can record when menu rows appear. You can track one background line. You can compare edge brightness and shadow strength.

Not proven by visible evidence alone

You cannot name the exact spring constants, shader code, number of render layers, sampling texture, blur kernel, or internal coordinate system merely by watching the animation.

What evidence would reduce the uncertainty? Official implementation documentation, framework source code, private application source code, graphics traces, performance captures, or controlled measurements could support a stronger claim. The unknown does not block the Android design. Our implementation can target the visible behavior and then be measured on the Pixel 8 Pro.

8 · Android connection

No Android source change in Lesson 1.2

The first Android project is created in Lesson 1.3.

Why we are not coding the menu yet

The transition depends on shapes, coordinates, backdrop pixels, refraction, lighting, and motion. Those concepts are taught later. Starting with the final menu would hide the reason behind most lines of code.

What this observation work becomes later

The stage notes become visual acceptance criteria. For example: “During middle expansion, the container is larger, the source-side edge remains anchored, and the menu rows are still hidden.” Later code must satisfy that statement.

Exercise 2 · Diagnostic

Exercise 4

A straight background line appears bent near the moving boundary. What can you claim with confidence? What additional evidence would you need before claiming that an AGSL shader caused the bend?

Write all four answers before copying.
Answer 1

A frame is one complete image at one moment. A stage is a human-chosen label for a useful group of nearby frames that share a visible purpose.

Answer 2

No. The stage is an analysis label. A smooth animation can pass continuously through the frames that we call middle expansion without stopping.

Answer 3

Visible fact: the boundary is larger than the source control and the rows are hidden. Implementation hypothesis: the software may delay the row opacity animation until enough space exists. The second statement needs code, tracing, or measurement to prove.

Answer 4

You can claim that the line’s visible position or shape differs near the boundary. You cannot identify AGSL from the pixels alone. Stronger evidence would include source code, a graphics trace that shows a runtime shader, official documentation, or a controlled experiment that isolates the effect.
