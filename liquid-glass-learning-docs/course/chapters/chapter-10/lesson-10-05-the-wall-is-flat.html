Lesson 10.5 — The wall is flat and stands straight up

Every point of the wall is 0.302 to the right of S, so the arrow between two of its points goes 0 to the right, and the straight line between them stays on the wall. Light theme · desktop · works from file://.

In Lesson 10.4 the walk to the tip of N ended on a wall: all points 0.302 to the right of S. This lesson shows why that wall is flat: the arrow between two of its points has x part 0, so the straight line between them never leaves the wall. It also stands straight up, for a similar reason: a move up has x part 0 (Lesson 10.4 showed it from Gx; it holds from any point of the wall). Lesson 10.6 draws a straight line on this wall and turns it.

New here
  • Flat: a surface is flat when the straight line between any two of its points stays on it (not curved; not “level”). Step 2.
  • P, Q and M: two points of the wall, and a point of the straight line between them, P plus a share of the arrow from P to Q (Lesson 5.4's progress). Step 3.
  • The one idea: the wall is flat. Any two of its points are both 0.302 to the right of S, so the arrow between them has x part 0.302 − 0.302 = 0; every point of the straight line between them is the first point plus a share of that arrow, so it is 0.302 + 0 = 0.302 to the right of S: on the wall (Steps 3–4).
  • Step 4 adds that the wall stands straight up: Lesson 10.4's fact that a move up keeps you on the wall, from Gx, holds from any point of the wall.

“Flat” in this lesson means not curved (Step 2); the 2D pictures are called views or drawings. “Flat top” is an older name in which flat means level. A point of the glass or the floor has x, y and height in UV; points of the wall are in arrow units from S. An arrow has an x part, a y part and a height part, counted from its own start. The wall and the points on it are helpers in arrow units, counted from S: places in the zoomed drawing, not sizes on the glass. Polar coordinates are not used.

Exercise 1 — How far to the right of S is the middle of the straight line from P₁ to P₂?

Why this question. The middle of the line is a point that neither P₁ nor P₂ is. Is it on the wall too?

What you do. Find the arrow from P₁ to P₂ and its x part. Then add half of that arrow to P₁.

Question: how far to the right of S is the middle of the straight line from P₁ to P₂?

Show the answer
  • The arrow from P₁ to P₂: x part 0.302 − 0.302 = 0, y part 0.6 − (−0.3) = 0.9, height part 0.9 − 0.1 = 0.8.
  • Half of it: x part 0.5 × 0 = 0, y part 0.5 × 0.9 = 0.45, height part 0.5 × 0.8 = 0.4.
  • The middle, from S: x 0.302 + 0 = 0.302, y −0.3 + 0.45 = 0.15, height 0.1 + 0.4 = 0.5.
  • So the middle is 0.302 to the right of S: on the wall, seen edge-on as a line in this view, like P₁ and P₂.
Exercise 2 — Which of the straight lines a, b and c stays on the wall all the way?

Why this question. All three lines start on the wall. Does a line that starts on the wall always stay on it?

What you do. For each line, find the x part of the arrow from P₃ to its end. Then ask where its middle is.

Question: which of the straight lines a, b and c stays on the wall all the way?

Show the answer
  • For a line from P₃, only an end that is also on the wall gives an arrow with x part 0.
  • The middle of each line is P₃ plus half of its arrow (Lesson 9.9):
    • line a: x part 0.302 − 0.302 = 0; middle 0.302 + 0 = 0.302: on the wall.
    • line b: x part 0.452 − 0.302 = 0.15; half 0.5 × 0.15 = 0.075; middle 0.302 + 0.075 = 0.377: off it.
    • line c: x part 0 − 0.302 = −0.302; half 0.5 × (−0.302) = −0.151; middle 0.302 + (−0.151) = 0.151: off it.
  • share × 0.15 (or share × (−0.302)) is not 0 for any share above 0, so lines b and c leave the wall right after P₃.
  • Line a runs along the wall, seen edge-on as a line in this view.
