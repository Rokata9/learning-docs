Shortcut — Safari's “…” button becomes the menu

A side lesson next to the course: it does not count toward the 216 lessons. iPhone 16 Pro, iOS 26, Safari. Light theme · desktop · works from file://.

1 · Upside-down text at the edge2 · Read points3 · The rim in 3D 4 · Push and fold5 · unsettled6 · One pixel, traced7 · Lab 8 · Code9 · Challenge10 · Exercises11 · Metal exercise12 · Record

On iOS 26, a tap on Safari's “…” button turns the button into the menu: a piece of glass leaves the button, flies up, grows, and stops as the menu. Look at the top edge of the flying glass in the zooms below: parts of the menu text there are shown upside down, as if the text were folded over the edge. This lesson explains that fold (Sections 1–4) and why it is strong only while the glass flies (Section 5).

What differs between the two pictures. The title of one history row is painted over in the recording, for privacy; this page writes a made-up title there. This page draws simple shapes instead of Apple's icons. The two glasses differ by at most 6.5 pt at the measured moments (Section 5, step 4, shows them).

Section 1 · the idea in picturesThe glass shows the menu text upside down near its edge

What the glass shows: three pictures

Safari makes what you see inside the glass in two steps. The three pictures show the moment t = 0.213 s, the bottom of the screen, all at the same scale. The white box in each picture marks the zoom under it.

1The page
The page is Safari's own screen behind the menu: the list of pages you visited, the Edit button and the toolbar. No glass is drawn.

2The glass picture
The glass picture is a flat picture made for the glass: the page blurred, the dark glass color over it, and the menu text on top. It is drawn here inside the outline (violet), with no lens: the text is upright and whole.
the lens
3The output
The output is what the screen shows. Inside the outline, the lens gives each pixel the color of another spot of the glass picture (Section 2). Near the top edge the bottoms of the letters are shown twice: upside down in the pink band, then upright below it. So the letters look folded over the edge.
  • blue: the tops of the letters, never shown in the output. yellow → orange → red: the bottoms of the letters (15.1 to 18.2 pt below the top edge); the output shows them twice.
Why the zoom of the output looks like a plain stretched “Tab”. The band above the white fold line shows only the bottoms of the letters, upside down. An upside-down letter bottom looks like a letter top, so the band and the upright part below the fold line join into what looks like one stretched word. Two things give it away: the T has lost its crossbar (the tops of the letters are never shown), and with the colors on, the band runs red → yellow down to the fold line and yellow → red after it.

The menu text is drawn sharp in these three pictures, so that you can follow the letters. In Safari it is also blurred while the glass flies (see the recording); the blur does not change where the pixels read.

          Section 4 · the length of each arrowWhere the glass is low, the push is long: the fold

          The length of each arrow comes from h: push = edgePush × (1 − h ÷ bevel). edgePush = 24 pt is the push on the outline. bevel = 28 pt is the width of the rim and the height of the flat top (Section 3). So the push is longest where the glass is lowest. Why: a real piece of glass bends the view behind it most at its edge, where its surface is steep. In its flat middle it does not bend it at all. This rule copies that look with one number per pixel. (Apple's own formula is not public: this one was fitted by eye to the recording.) Press Next.

            The fold, one pixel at a time

              How the exact fold line, 6.741 pt, is founddrag this bar to move the window
              1. At the fold line the read point stops moving back. So there, for 1 pt further in, the push loses exactly 1 pt.
              2. The dashed line touches the rim at H. It is at a right angle to the radius K–H (true for every circle). So its small triangle has the shape of K–A–H turned a quarter turn: h along, leg up.
              3. So near H the rim climbs leg ÷ h pt for each pt inward. At the fold line: 21.259 ÷ 18.222 = 1.167 pt per pt (in the picture: 4 pt inward, it climbs 4 × 1.167 ≈ 4.67 pt).
              4. push = 24 × (1 − h ÷ 28). Each pt that h climbs takes 24 ÷ 28 = 0.857 pt from the push.
              5. So for each pt inward the push loses 0.857 × (leg ÷ h). At the fold line this is 1: 0.857 × 1.167 = 1.000. In whole numbers: leg ÷ h = 28 ÷ 24.
              6. So leg = 28 × k and h = 24 × k for one number k. K–A–H is a right triangle with long side 28 (Section 3), so (28 × k)² + (24 × k)² = 28². That is k² × (784 + 576) = 784, so k = 28 ÷ √1360.
                28 × 28 = 78424 × 24 = 576784 + 576 = 1360
                √1360 = 36.87818k = 28 ÷ 36.87818 = 0.759257
              7. The numbers (more decimals here, because the differences are small):
                leg = 28 × 0.759257 = 21.25920inside = 28 − 21.25920 = 6.74080 → 6.741: the fold line

              Section 5 · the math ideaStrong only while it flies: unsettled

              At rest, the menu has a weak rim: bevel 14 pt, edgePush 6 pt. Its upside-down band is only 1.1 pt wide and lies over the empty glass above the text, so nothing looks folded. While the glass flies, the rim is the strong rim of Sections 3 and 4: bevel 28 pt, edgePush 24 pt. One number moves the rim between the two: unsettled, 0 at rest and near 1 in the middle of the flight. It comes from two springs. Press Next, and drag the time.

                The lens: SafariMenuGlass.metal

                /// How far the lens moves the read point inward, for a pixel sd from the outline.
static float rimPushAmount(float sd, float bevel, float edgePush) {
    if (bevel <= 0.0) { return 0.0; }
    float inside = clamp(-sd, 0.0, bevel);                 // how far inside the outline, at most bevel
    float leg = bevel - inside;                            // from K, the rim circle's center (Section 3)
    float h = sqrt(max(bevel * bevel - leg * leg, 0.0));   // the height of the rim (Section 3)
    return edgePush * (1.0 - h / bevel);                   // new: edgePush on the outline, 0 on the flat top
}

[[ stitchable ]] half4 safariMenuGlass(float2 position, SwiftUI::Layer picture, float4 box, ... float2 lens, ...) {
    float sd = glassSd(position, box, cornerRadius, drop); // Lesson 9.4, joined to the drop at the button
    ...

                How Swift attaches the shader. The glass picture gets two modifiers. .compositingGroup() (Lesson 6.3) first flattens its parts (the blurred page, the color, the text) into one picture; without it, SwiftUI would run the shader once for each part. Then .layerEffect(ShaderLibrary.safariMenuGlass(…), maxSampleOffset: …) new runs the shader for every pixel and passes the flattened picture as picture. maxSampleOffset tells SwiftUI the largest distance, along x and along y, between a pixel and the points it reads, so that SwiftUI draws enough of the picture around the view. Here a read point is at most edgePush = 24 pt away from its pixel. Every frame, a TimelineView (Lesson 5.2) asks the motion for new numbers and passes them as the shader's arguments (Lesson 8.9).

                Browser and iPhone. This page runs the same lines: its motion code is a copy of SafariMenuMotion.swift that gives the same numbers (checked to 9 decimals for 436 moments), and its WebGL shader repeats SafariMenuGlass.metal. Three things differ. The page blurs with three box blurs in a row instead of Core Image's Gaussian. SwiftUI's .blur(radius:) of the menu text is drawn here with a Gaussian of half that radius: an estimate, matched by eye to the recording. And the browser draws text with its own font renderer and draws simple shapes instead of Apple's icons. The 3D pictures are only for seeing: the iPhone never draws a 3D glass; the shader only computes h.

                Section 10 · exercisesExercises

                Each exercise starts with a picture. Read the question, pick one choice, then open the answer. Every answer draws the reason.

                Exercise 1 — at rest, how wide is the upside-down band?

                The change. The glass is at rest, so unsettled = 0 and the rim is the weak rim: bevel = 14, edgePush = 6 (Section 5). The push line stays push = edgePush × (1 − h ÷ bevel).

                What it checks. The upside-down band runs from the outline to the fold line (Section 4, the fold, steps 4–5). Its lower end is the pixel whose read point is the highest one. The picture shows the weak rim, from the side, with one arrow every 0.5 pt.

                What you do. Find the pixel where the read points stop moving up and start moving down again.

                Question: at rest, how wide is the upside-down band?

                Show the answer

                Exercise 2 — does a straight-ramp rim show anything upside down?

                The change. The rim becomes a straight slope: h = inside. So the push line becomes push = edgePush * (1.0 - inside / bevel), with the strong rim: bevel = 28, edgePush = 24.

                What it checks. Section 4, the fold, steps 2–4: a read point moves back when the push shrinks by more than 1 pt while the pixel moves 1 pt inward.

                What you do. Read the picture: the teal line is the push of the ramp, the navy curve the push of the quarter circle. For E (inside 0) and E₁ (inside 1), compute where they read: read = inside + push.

                Question: does a straight-ramp rim show anything upside down?

                Show the answer

                Why: for each pt the pixel moves inward, the ramp's push shrinks by 24 ÷ 28 = 0.857 pt, so the read point still moves inward by 1 − 0.857 = 0.143 pt. No arrows cross, so nothing is upside down: the stripes 24 and 27 are shown upright and stretched over the whole rim. A ramp would flip the order only if edgePush were larger than bevel.

                Exercise 3 — why does the closing glass first jump up?

                What it checks. Section 5: the gap between the springs drives the lift.

                What you do. Read travel and lead at J (t = 0.047 s). Compute the gap, then compare the lift (36 pt × unsettled) with how far the straight path has come down.

                The check: how the app compares your picture with the key's

                The screen draws three pictures: yours, the key's, and the check. The check is built in two steps, both new here.

                1. .blendMode(.difference). Lesson 3.10 showed blend modes: a rule for how a picture drawn on top mixes with the color under it. difference is one more rule: for each channel (red, green, blue, each 0 … 1) the result is the top value minus the bottom value, without its sign: |top − bottom|. The app draws your picture, then the key's picture on top of it with this rule. Where the two pictures have the same color, every channel gives 0: black.

                2. markDifference (a prepared shader in chapter10/ExerciseCheck.metal) then looks at each pixel of that result. If any channel is more than 2/255 (2 steps of the 0 … 255 scale of Lesson 3.1), it paints the pixel red; otherwise dark gray. 2/255 lets tiny rounding differences pass (the same formula written in another order can differ in the last digits).

                float rimPush(float sd, float bevel, float edgePush) {
    float inside = clamp(-sd, 0.0, bevel);
    float leg = bevel - inside;
    float h = sqrt(bevel * bevel - leg * leg);
    return edgePush * (1.0 - h / bevel);
}

float2 foldReadPoint(float2 position, float4 box, float cornerRadius, float bevel, float edgePush) {
    float sd = exerciseGlassSd(position, box, cornerRadius);
    float e = 0.5;
    float2 slope = float2(
        exerciseGlassSd(position + float2(e, 0.0), box, cornerRadius) - exerciseGlassSd(position - float2(e, 0.0), box, cornerRadius),
        exerciseGlassSd(position + float2(0.0, e), box, cornerRadius) - exerciseGlassSd(position - float2(0.0, e), box, cornerRadius));
    float slopeLength = length(slope);
    float2 outward = slopeLength > 0.0 ? slope / slopeLength : float2(0.0);
    float push = rimPush(sd, bevel, edgePush);
    return position - outward * push;
}

                Why it works: rimPush is Section 3's height and Section 4's push in one function. foldReadPoint is Section 2's arrow: on a straight side the outward direction is (0, −1) or (−1, 0), and around a corner it points away from the corner circle's center (T3: the 3-4-5 direction). In the middle the push is 0, so the picture is not moved there.
