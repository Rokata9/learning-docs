# AGENTS.md — Liquid Glass Learning Documents

The rulebook for the interactive learning documents. Keep this file short. Merge new rules into existing ones. Delete superseded rules. A rulebook too long to remember is worse than a short one.

## Rule 0 — short lessons in plain words (learner, 2026-11-05). It wins over every rule below.

The learner: “It doesn't make sense to overcomplicate and make over-verbose everything just for the sake of it, if a lesson can be just a few sentences, a diagram and exercises.” Lessons 10.10–10.12 failed this: 1,870 words of page text and 8,658 with tooltips for “half a turn puts a minus on both numbers”.

- **Cut the text, never the pictures** (learner, 2026-11-05: “keep graphics as they are, don't nerf them, they're fine”). Keep the hero pictures, every stepper picture, Section 2's draggable picture and the exercise pictures as rich as the rules below make them. The cut is in the words:
  - the hero: a title, one or two sentences that say where the lesson leads, and a “Where this is used” line: the later lessons and the glass effect that use the result (learner, 2026-11-08: “no mention on where this material be used”);
  - a **Uses** box: one line per earlier fact, with lesson links; no “New here” essay;
  - each stepper caption: at most 5 short lines that say what the picture shows now; no “Why ask?” lines, no re-introduction of objects the labels already name;
  - Section 2: the heading, one line, and a table whose text cells hold names and numbers, not sentences;
  - exercises: the question, the choices and a 2–4 line hidden answer;
  - ⓘ text: a recall of one fact in one to three lines; a number chain may be longer.
- **Length:** a granular lesson, which is most of the course, has about 300 words of page text before the exercises; picture labels and tooltips are not counted. A more complex lesson may use more text where the idea needs it (learner, 2026-11-05). The extra text still follows this rule: plain words, each fact once, no recaps. It does not bring back the old shape: no “New here” essay, no “Why ask?” lines, no restated conclusions.
- **Standard math notation:** point names are capital letters (P, Q, P′ for the image of P). Write segment AB, its length |AB|, line AB, angle ∠ABC and triangle △ABC. Never write “the line from A to B”, “the distance between A and B”, “the teal line” or “lime: S, M, X” when a two-letter name or △SMX works; a point the pictures label with a letter (the tip of L, labelled L) goes by that letter in these names: SL, never “S to the tip of L” (learner, 2026-11-09, on 10.26). Every number says what it is counted from: a point's numbers “from S”, an arrow's parts from its own start, a distance by its two points (|ML| = 0.510, never a bare 0.510 label). A segment, such as a triangle's side, has one number, its length; only a point or an arrow has three, so where SM is a side, the walk with three parts is “the arrow from S to M”, never SM.
- **Plain words:** say it the way a teacher says it at the board: “T is on line n”, “higher than Gx”, “the upper half of the circle”. Never build a phrase out of course nouns (“lay the teal line along every line”, “up from Gx or level with it”, “none is lain along”, “passes 0.953 once”, “all of N's going along L is S to F”, “N is N at K, as above” all failed; so did “GP′ is GP pointing the other way” and “X lands on the tip of L”, learner 2026-11-08). Say where a point ends up (P′ is on line PG, on the other side of G, as far from G as P; the flip moves X to the tip of L), and give an opposite arrow's parts in direction words (0.077 left becomes 0.077 right). If a sentence must be read twice, rewrite it.
- **Prove only what is not visible.** A fact that the figure shows at a glance gets one sentence and the figure, never a proof in words. Examples: a line through the center crosses the circle twice; half a turn points the other way.
- **Calculations name each number once.** Write a square with its number (“0.779² = 0.779 × 0.779”). Never reuse a name, such as “x part²”, for a different number a line later. Add no “check” line for a value the page itself placed (T measured 0.953 from Gx): learner, 2026-11-05, on 10.12's readout.
- **Say each conclusion once.** Do not repeat it in the title, the lead, the idea, a heading and a table.
- The rules below still apply where they add no text:
  - each figure names its coordinate system once;
  - numbers are exact;
  - points are clickable, and labels sit inside the picture;
  - no eye-angle guidance;
  - the pictures stay as they are.

## Scope and files

- Course home: `liquid-glass-course.html`. Chapter hub: `lesson-[chapter-name].html`. Sub-lesson: `lesson-[chapter-number]-[lesson-number]-[lesson-name].html`.
- Every lesson is one standalone HTML file that works from `file://`: inline CSS, JavaScript, SVG. No external libraries or CDNs.
- iOS source lives in the sibling repo `/Users/rosen/Code/LiquidGlassLab-iOS`. Never create `ios/` or `android/` here. When a revision changes iOS code, state the exact Swift target path; when it changes none, say so. Only B and E lessons ship Swift or Metal files, wired into the chapter screen and built, in the same session; a C lesson ships its HTML page only. Without the lab or Xcode, tell the learner before generating a B or E lesson instead of shipping an uncompiled excerpt.
- No ZIP archives unless the learner asks. Edit the two repos in place. No archive folder (learner, 2026-11-04): the page from before a split stays in git only, and the handoff names it as `git show <commit>:<path>`.
- Desktop only: verify at `1920×1080` or larger. Light theme only for lessons.
- Do not store lesson completion counts, checkpoints, generated frontiers, or completion/generation status in project files or course pages. Report the learner-confirmed count only in chat when generating a new lesson. The iOS demo selects the latest chapter on a cold launch and brings its chapter chip into view.

## Language — the top rule set

1. Plain English for a non-native speaker. Short sentences. One idea per sentence. No idioms. A stepper caption is a short list: one sentence per line, about 14 words at most, and at most about 10 lines; a step that needs more is two steps. A line that announces items (“two things:”, “Compare with …:”, “Repeat Steps 6–8:”) carries those items as nested sub-points under it, never as lines at its own level. A reason gets its own line, as a question and its answer (“Why a board? Near one pixel, the glass is almost flat.”). Never fold a reason and a comparison with an earlier step into one sentence (“We use a board because …, as the rim was … in Section 1, Step 9” failed); point at a picture on the same screen instead. A colour name used as an object name ("the light-green curve") must mean one object in the whole lesson. Never chain several quantities in one sentence (“one small step further in adds the step and takes away what the push loses, so …” failed): show the change as a picture with colored numbers instead.
2. Define every term at first use, in the same sentence. Notation is a term too: before numbers in brackets such as (−3, 1) or a part letter such as z appear, a drawn example says what each position means (x part, height part). A pair read on a sheet's own rulers (along 0.860, across −0.510) draws both rulers, each with its 0 and its plus and minus sides, where it first appears, and the reference page lists the form (10.26, learner 2026-11-08). Add a small `ⓘ` tooltip for anything that may need a refresher: an older formula, an older lesson's object, a chosen constant. Tooltips carry reminders only; the page must be fully understandable without opening them. A word that could describe a graph or the picture (linear, straight, smooth) must say which one it describes. Each section re-anchors the objects it draws with labels in its first picture (a legend only for marks too small to label, rule 17); never rely on a definition from another section. Do not describe how the page computes a value (search, bisection) unless the learner needs it. A lesson may assume only earlier lessons in curriculum order and any prerequisite the learner explicitly names in the current conversation; an idea from a later lesson counts as new, even if a side lesson sits next to it. Before writing, grep the prerequisite lesson files for every term, formula, constant and code construct the lesson uses; explain each one that is not there, in this lesson, where it first appears, with the numbers (a tuning constant, a font measure, a time constant: say what it is and where it comes from; for a tuning constant also say whether it is a property of the material or a chosen dial (a chosen number in a formula, such as the walk of 1 that becomes the height part 1, is called “chosen” where it is used, with what another choice would give), and show what other values would do). Put a **Uses** box near the top (Rule 0): the earlier facts used, one line each, with lesson links. A code excerpt gets a line-by-line table (line · what it does · taught in) that marks every construct the prerequisite lessons do not teach as new and explains it in that row. Banned words: `arctan`, “lattice”, “level-set”, “X is not an input”. “Amount” means the Lesson 7.10 slider until a later lesson redefines it.
3. One object, one name. Never rename a thing mid-lesson. A word keeps one meaning: along and across are a point's two numbers (10.27); an arrow's parts along and across are a second use, defined where they first appear (the end's number minus the start's) with both points' numbers drawn (10.29's unexplained “part along” lines, learner 2026-11-09). A point has x, y and height: where it is, counted from the origin of the coordinates. An arrow has an x part, a y part and a height part: how far it goes, counted from its own start. Never write an arrow's number as a position (“1 in x”, “height −0.45”, “(x, height) = (0.45, 1)”), and where two arrows are on screen each part names its arrow (the x walk's x part 1, rawNormal's x part 0.45): a turn makes the x walk's height part into rawNormal's x part. A table that holds arrows has plain direction headings (to the right, toward the bottom of the picture, up; minus: the opposite way) and names each row's start and end (GP: from G to P), never x / y / height columns (10.25's table put points and arrows under them; learner, 2026-11-08). An arrow named after a direction (“x walk”) says, where it is defined, whether it lies along that direction; the x walk on a tilted board is not along x (x is always level). Keep the names of a quantity's parts the same from the section that introduces them to the end (x part, y part, height part, shadow, length); code names (rawNormal.x, .z, x², z²) appear only in the code sections (code excerpt, coding challenge, Metal exercise), translated where they first appear (.x = x part, .z = height part), and every formula outside them uses the part names (√(x part² + y part² + height part²), never √(x² + y² + height²)), and a length computed again later uses the same steps as when it was introduced (shadow first, then length). A traced-value card applies a recipe that an earlier section built; it does not redraw the helper objects of that derivation (the walks) unless its own lines use them: card 4 drew and computed two walks that no later line used, and the learner asked why they were there. Show the result's meaning with objects already on screen instead (rawNormal's square corners with the two glass curves, in the front and side views). Name a chosen number by what it is, “height part = 1 (chosen, always 1)”, never by its history (“the turned walk of 1”). A view of an object is not a new object: write “rawNormal, seen from the front: (0.45, 1)”, never a coined “front arrow” or “side arrow”. A result on its way to an object is not a new object either: name the one object when the goal is set, and let its parts appear as they are found (“turned x walk (0.45, 0, 1)” was the rejected “front arrow” under a new name). No metaphor names such as seat, stick, landing, net, or depth: call the object by its plain name — pixel, arrow, output position, the box's height. A place on a line is named by that line's ruler (“on the minus side of S”, “F is at the tip of N”), never by a coined direction word (behind, in front of, backward), and two points at one place are “F is at the tip of N”, never “F is the tip of N” (learner, 2026-11-02). A point made by a construction (F: where a line from the tip of N meets L's line at a square corner) is never described by where one picture puts it (“below N”); when a control moves it to a special place, the step says why it lands there and a picture shows it on the way. “Level” means parallel to the floor; a line drawn horizontally in a flat picture is “drawn left to right”. A direction word (along, across) names the line or circle it follows, defined and drawn in the same state. Where rises or falls meets a direction on the screen, say which is which: “falls toward the top of the picture” was read as a contradiction, because the top sounds high. Never change the case of text that can hold a name or symbol (no `text-transform: uppercase`: it turned ρ into Ρ, read as P, and e into E). Table headings use the picture's names (W.x, not point.x), typeset formulas as math, and say in words what each symbol is (e: the distance from C to P). Never add a second word for a quantity that already has a name: “depth” and “deeper” for Section 2's inside confused the learner; use inside, “further inside” and “nearer the outline”. A reference line such as the top edge is defined once with a picture (and its coordinate) before any step measures from it, and a picture that turns a vertical line on its side shows that turn. When a name needs a mental translation before the picture makes sense, use the visible role instead: “upside-down copy” rather than “copy 1”, “original-order copy” rather than “upright”, “the line y = 0.50” rather than “pixel row” or “glass-picture row” (never “row”, “column” or “cut along the row”: the learner found “row” unclear across several lessons), and “top of the glass” rather than “top edge”. Do not use band/width for a region whose meaningful measurement is its height. A side view of the glass is a “slice along the line y = …” (or x = …); the first slice in a lesson is introduced with a 3D picture: the line on the screen, the glass cut straight down along it with the front half removed, and the cut face, beside the flat slice. Every slice prints values on its axis (x 0.65, y 0.381: outline). A slice whose line does not pass through C carries a small from-above map with its line, C and the flat-top circle, and says which parts it cuts (the line x = 0.71 cuts only the rim; its middle is y = 0.50, not C).
4. Never point back vaguely. Forbidden patterns: "between the two", "the former", "that value", "those moves", "other points", "the same amounts", "near one pixel" (name the pixel: W, and the patch: from above A to above B), and it / its / this / they when the noun is not in the same sentence. Repeat the object's name instead. Points used in a claim get names in the picture (P₂, P₃) and their numbers in the table, so “the same” is shown as two equal results.
5. Every purpose statement is followed by its reason in the same or the next sentence. "We multiply by falloff **because** …" Nothing is taken for granted. A result never comes before its reason: no formula now with a later "check" that is really the derivation (x part = −slopeX stated in Step 6, the turn that gives it shown in Steps 7–8, read as “suddenly”). A drawn helper (legs, construction lines) is a purpose too: the caption says which number it gives and which formula needs that number, and the picture draws it whole, its start point included. A helper that leaves the surface it measures says why, in its label and caption (a level leg above a tilted ramp: “level, in the air above the board; the walk itself lies on the board”). A helper drawn at only some objects (circles at two of nine pixels) says why those; otherwise draw it at the step's own object. When a lesson picks a specific tool where an obvious simpler one exists (smoothstep over a straight line), state why the simple one fails and show the comparison picture: an in-place toggle on content that reveals the difference, with a magnifier when the difference is small. Say with a number how big the difference is and on which content it shows.

## Visuals first

6. A picture carries the explanation; prose captions the picture. Every picture shows where each of its points comes from (an output pixel joined to its read point; the line from C through it; a moved point's way from its start, so a turn or a flip is never drawn only as its start and its end: 10.29's two lime triangles at 0° and 180° read as “rotated and translated”, learner 2026-11-09; a turn's way also labels its middle and how far the point stays from it, and points that turn together are drawn at the same angle: 10.29's two half circles of different sizes, middles unlabelled, read as a turn “around 2 points”). The traced value's 3D view is drawn like Section 1's, with the same zoom and the same reduced scene; when a drag changes the studied shape's size, the scale follows it, so the shape keeps its size on screen. A crowded picture is fixed before delivery, never named as an open item (10.29's Section 2 at scale 112 put six labels within 80 px of S; learner, 2026-11-09: “Why it is so crowded”), prefers the real scene to scale with many lines over a symmetric schematic, and stands beside at most two sentences; never print a derived number without saying which direction or object it belongs to. If a paragraph describes something a picture could show, draw the picture. Prefer interactive pictures (toggle, drag, slider, stepper) over paragraphs. Show examples instead of listing them: if the text names real content (text rows, icons), a picture shows it. When an effect moves or reshapes content, let the learner pick a piece of it (a word, a cell): tint it in the source, give every output pixel that reads it the same tint, and keep the tint while the control moves. Draw the effect large enough to see at a glance. When an explanation depends on where pixels read, draw it with arrows from output pixels to their read points, stacked the way the read really moves: with C above the read moves up (wallpaper on top, output below, arrows up); under the straight top of the glass it moves down (output on top, original picture below, arrows down). Add marks for what the arrows show (×2 for a source pixel read twice, ✕ for one never read). Its caption is at most four short sentences; tables of numbers stay visible under the picture, never in a collapsed box (the learner reads the calculations beside the picture). When the read points and their pixels are far apart, draw one window of the screen split by a black line (the wallpaper on C's side, the output beyond, one shared scale) so lines from C and the arrows run straight across it; do not split it into pictures plus magnifiers whose frames look like objects. Start from the effect the learner can see: the first picture zooms on it and marks it with a labelled bracket, and a caption names an effect (mirrored, folded) only when that picture shows it at a glance. When the real scene is busy or moving, study the effect first on a still test case built to make it readable (stripes numbered by their distance from the edge, each with an arrow that turns around when the stripe is shown upside down), then return to the real scene. A chain of pictures that produces the output (page → glass picture → output) is drawn as pictures side by side joined by arrows, each with its name as its title. When the lesson relies on an imagined 3D shape (a surface height), give a turnable 3D view (drag to turn, wheel to zoom, preset views), cut along the line the lesson follows, beside the flat side view of that cut at the same scale; arrows go from the surface point above a pixel to its read point, so the direction of every push is drawn. Before the lesson reasons in 3D, it introduces the view in its own steps: the flat view from above first, then a tilt that adds height (one point given as three numbers). The page never turns a 3D view between steps: one view angle from the tilt step on, and a turn by the learner stays until the learner resets it; a flat view goes in a flat picture beside the 3D one. Every 3D picture carries a small corner box: its coordinates, the x, y and height arrows as drawn, and what is in arrow units (arrows, lengths, rulers) and what 1 is (1 = N's length; learner, 2026-11-08). A zoomed view shows its whole floor patch, with the words on the floor's direction arrows (right, toward the top of the picture, toward the bottom of the picture), in every state of its controls; a page built from an earlier page's engine keeps both (learner, 2026-11-07: 10.12–10.20 had switched the words off, and 10.16–10.20's camera left the floor out of the picture). When a flat picture does an operation and a 3D picture shows only its result, the caption says so. No eye-position guidance anywhere: no eye icons, phone or monitor metaphors, “stand at …”, “your eye”, or “looking down 30°” (the learner found it unhelpful, 2026-10-30). The tilt step says that x, y and height are fixed directions (x toward the right edge, y toward the bottom edge, height up) and that turning the view changes only their drawing; a point above a pixel keeps the pixel's x and y. Before the first single-view argument, one step shows one walk with its three numbers and the same walk from above, from the front and from the side, each small picture saying which two numbers it shows and which one it does not show. Name views plainly (view from above, front view, side view) by the two directions each shows, never “look along y”. A part along the third direction is “not shown in this view”; when a drawn object still shows a line in that view, say which of its parts the line is (in the front view the y walk (0, 1, −0.60) is a line straight down: its −0.60); never write “you cannot see any y part” beside a visible y-walk line. A walk or arrow is never presented as a second coordinate system: it is the change of the same x, y and height from its start to its end. A 3D step that repeats a 2D argument shows that 2D picture beside it, drawn as in the earlier section. After the lesson has introduced 3D, pictures of the surface are turnable 3D views in UV (learner request 2026-10-30: “only 3D from now on, that's the real deal”): zoom on the traced point, draw a small floor patch with its UV range, the two lines through the point, the glass surface along them as 3D curves, posts for heights and arrows from H with a stated arrow ruler; draw no whole-glass mesh at that zoom (it buries the posts) and say in a label where C lies. Flat side views stay only where a section teaches the side view itself. Before placing an effect label (upside down, mirrored), look at the pixels under it: if the effect can look like something else on real content (upside-down letter bottoms look like letter tops, so a folded word looks merely stretched), color the source by position (letters by their distance from the edge) so the output shows the reversal, and say in one sentence why it is easy to miss. When the lesson says the real recording shows an effect, show the clearest real frame beside this page's sharp render of the same moment, mark on the frame the features that prove the effect (a b closed at both ends), say why the frame cannot show more (resolution, blur, only part of the letters folded), and state any mismatch between this page and the frame with its size. A draggable pixel gets the full chain for that pixel, every line in order (its sd, inside, h, push, outward from the four neighbor sd values, read point, and the color found there), so the learner can follow any position, not only the teaching one. Show that chain in a draggable window that opens when the learner clicks a point of the output (closes with ✕, Escape, or a click elsewhere), not as a permanent panel that pushes the pictures off the screen. Arrows that overlap and cross do not explain themselves: first give each element its own row (one row per pixel: the pixel, its arrow, where it lands), with the change from the row above as colored chips (+1 moved · − push lost · = read point moved), and only then show them together. Show a consequence on an object whose mirror image looks different (a color ramp, a triangle that becomes an hourglass, an L that becomes a [), never on symmetric stripes. When the real content's folded part looks like itself (a b's bottom strip, so the folded b reads as a stretched b), say so, and draw beside it a letter whose fold is unmistakable and the same letter only stretched. No gray text blocks of reasoning or calculation: a readout holds numbers in a table with colored columns that match the picture; reasoning goes in short captions beside the picture that shows it, and an optional derivation goes into a window with its own picture.
7. Label every drawn element inside the picture: rings, arrows, points, regions. If a label does not fit, put a numbered tag on the object and name the tag in the adjacent text on the same screen. Every chart says in one sentence beside it what one bar or mark stands for. The reader must never guess which drawn object a number or sentence refers to. When a slider or drag can produce shapes the lesson has not named (turned-around words, loops), a picture next to the control names each shape and draws where it comes from. A legend lists only what the current state draws (this step, this part); every letter in the state's table is drawn and labelled in its picture. Match parts of two pictures by marking the same piece names inside both pictures (colored bars with letters), not with ribbons or connectors drawn between them; the step's first sentence says what the step adds to the steps before it.
8. Text must match the picture exactly. A point is named only where the picture of that state draws and labels it: a point a control finds (the foot F) is drawn from the start, and a control's point (P) is defined, with the control that moves it, before any sentence uses it; a side picture that names lines (rulers of the x, y and height lines) needs those lines in the main picture beside it (10.19 failed all three, learner 2026-11-07: “random point naming that points don't exist”). A ruler reads at a point; a point does not read (“F reads more than P” failed). If the text says "three arrows", count the arrows in the picture before shipping. Plot only real values: draw a jump as two separate pieces, never as a vertical connector, and put no mark on a connector. Every point or object named in the text or a readout is drawn and labelled in every picture of that state, the 3D view included (10.26's M was only in the flat drawing, learner 2026-11-08), an implied one included (“the y part and the height part say the same for y and for height” needs the y line and the height line, as a selector on the picture), a ruler's numbers and its plus and minus sides are printed wherever the text uses them, and every distance, width or difference names its two points (from L to R) right where it is printed, and both points are drawn in the same picture (zoom out until both fit; a magnifier shows the small part). A caption that describes a motion names its direction, and every “first … then” order is checked in that direction. Read points are drawn on the wallpaper picture, never on an output picture that differs from it; a magnifier shows the same picture as its source. When a pixel's color is explained by where it reads, draw the whole chain: the pixel in the output, its spot and push arrow (with the push length printed) on the wallpaper, its read points, and one arrow per channel from each read point back to the pixel; pixels that share a line are shown one at a time with a chooser, never stacked. Print the push next to the small spacing it is compared with (0.10 versus 0.01).
9. A stepper is only for several facts that lead to one result. A lesson whose idea is its heading, a fact the picture shows, has no stepper: one picture in its final state, with its controls, and a caption of a few sentences (learner, 2026-11-05: “For things that obvious please remove the steppers, they're for explaining multiple facts leading to one”). Where a stepper is used: one new object or operation per step, and the picture must visibly change at every step. A caption-only change is a failed step. The picture must also render at full readable size at `1920×1080` (SVG labels ≥ 14 px on screen); a layout that squeezes a wide diagram into a narrow column fails. Use the fewest steps the causal chain needs, and let every control, calculation and picture read one shared state.
10. Every plotted point is clickable: a tooltip shows its name, role, and coordinates in their named coordinate space. Clicking a point must not move or select anything else; a click that also redraws (picks a letter) reopens its popup on the redrawn element. Support Enter/Space, close button, outside click, Escape. Keep the popup inside the viewport. Every point stays inside its picture: a zoom that follows points fits all of them, lowering its factor and printing the factor it uses. When real points are too close to activate, add an enlarged inset of the same picture and print its horizontal and vertical zoom factors; never move points to fake positions just to make them clickable. A clicked point says its point in a popup of a few short lines, not a panel. A dragged point's coordinates and result stay visible beside the picture, with its full calculation one `ⓘ` away. When the learner selects a point in the original picture, the arrows still run from its output pixels to it, in rule 6's read direction. Every point the learner can place or select (a traced pixel W, the points of a projection picture) is also draggable, in every picture that shows it (an enlarged inset included), and goes exactly where the pointer puts it (never snapped to a line; any rounding stays under one screen pixel): the matching points, arrows, coordinates and calculation follow the pointer live, and a press without a move stays a click. Offer a view of points evenly spaced along the whole shape with all their projections, spaced so no two dots overlap; show crowded ones in the enlarged inset, never hidden or moved. Images and the points drawn on them share one scale and origin. A draggable point's start place is chosen, and the line that first prints its numbers says so; every picture that shows the point draws each of its numbers as what it measures, the way the steps drew them for their own points (“along −0.200: from S to G” on segment SG, “across 0.350: from G to P” on GP), and these labels follow the drag (learner, 2026-11-09, on 10.27's Section 2: “how did you figure out it's −0.200 along??”). The same holds for any point whose numbers a step prints: draw its foot and each number as the lesson that defined them drew it (10.29's Step 2 printed Gx's and F₁'s along and across numbers with no foot and no segments; learner: “have nothing to do with the previous lesson”). A step draws only the objects its caption uses: a helper figure (Lesson 9.1's two legs) gets its own step, never drawn over another triangle (10.29's orange △GxDF₁ over the lime △SGxF₁; learner: “piling more garbage on top”). A step also draws only the objects its argument uses: a figure that only a later lesson needs is not the step's object (10.29's Step 1 drew the whole lime △SGxF₁, of which the calculation used only GxF₁; learner, 2026-11-09: “why were the triangles at step 1 necessary at all”).
11. Construction strokes need a dark halo plus a hue that is not in the picture: on the orange/navy checker cyan, lime, pink, violet, white; on a picture that itself shows red, yellow, cyan or blue (color fringes), violet, orange, pink, lime. Solid strokes, not dashes. Canvas labels take dark or light ink from the pixels under them; a label that crosses light and dark pixels sits on a light plate.

## Formulas — always fully expanded

12. Every displayed calculation shows, in order: the named formula → the substitution with every input visible → every intermediate value → the result → what consumes the result. Inputs known before the calculation starts (given, or found earlier) are gentle green (`.kn`: light green background, dark green text; on the dark tooltip, dark green background, light green text), everything worked out in it is plain, and the page says so once (learner, 2026-11-09). A generated chain marks them with `Trace.K(…)` per operand, never by matching the number's text (a computed 0.402 can equal a given 0.402). A hidden answer writes its whole calculation, the green inputs included, never only a result with an ⓘ (10.26's |SM₂| = √0.82 hid that the 0.82 was the parts squared and added, and that M₂'s numbers were already in the picture). Never print only a result. Each “=” does exactly one operation, and every line is rounded the same way (3 decimals by default; squares that feed a √ are exact), so a calculator reproduces every line — everywhere a number appears: text, generated readouts, tooltips, canvas labels, table cells. The reader never does arithmetic in their head outside the exercises. A number appears in the step that uses it, never earlier, not even in an ⓘ that points ahead (Step 1's ⓘ “Step 4 finds SM = (0.74, −0.30, 0.32) and |SM| = 0.860” failed), and each line of an ⓘ chain names its move (“to the right, half of it: −0.52 ÷ 2 = −0.26”; learner, 2026-11-08, on M's numbers printed in Step 1). A sign that changes is explained once, as the same fact as the formula's minus, never as a second sign rule beside it (“Why does b change sign?” beside −slopeX read as two rules): explain it where it changes: what the number measured before (a height: minus means down), what it measures after (an x: plus means right), and a check in words (the arrow leans downhill). Every number says what it measures and on which scale (a channel from 0 to 1, UV, pixels of a stated size). A slope or an arrow's part is a ratio, not a size on the object: where it can be read beside a real size (0.45 beside the bevel 0.10 was read as too big), print the real amounts (0.00698 UV down over 0.02 UV to the right) and compare slopes with slopes (the rim: 0.10 ÷ 0.10 = 1). This matters most when two different quantities print the same number: innerHalf.y = 0.20 − 0.10 = 0.10 was read as the corner radius 0.10, so name both beside the number and say the match holds only at this value. An arrow's label says which of its numbers it prints: its parts (0.45, 0.60, 1) or its length (“1.25 long”); a bare “rawNormal 1.25” beside “height part 1” was read as a height of 1.25. A count lists what was counted and by which rule (a table of the items, or numbered marks in the picture); a ratio says in words what it divides by what. Round only where rounding cannot change a comparison or a count (use more decimals for points closer than 0.001). In a walkthrough of many items (one row per pixel), every item shows its own full calculation right beside it, so the reader never has to remember a number from an earlier step. A comparison between two items is written in words with its subtraction (“push 6.357 shorter, pixel only 1 deeper: read point 6.357 − 1 = 5.357 pt nearer the outline”), never as signed chips such as “−5.357 back” or “+1 − 6.357 =”. When a ratio converts between two scales, draw both totals divided into the same number of equal parts, name the numerator’s total and the denominator’s part count, and trace one +1 input through the rule; never introduce the bare ratio. Use the original integers for a reciprocal (28 ÷ 24, not 1 ÷ a rounded 0.857143). Generated arithmetic must be calculator-exact from the displayed inputs at every slider position, not only at the teaching value.
13. Attach a `ⓘ` provenance icon to any value that comes from elsewhere: an earlier lesson, a slider, a fixed constant, a previous step. An ⓘ obeys rule 8 too: each number it prints is drawn in that step's pictures, or the ⓘ leaves it out (10.29's Step 2 ⓘ “Here Gx is 0.302 on the x line's ruler and F₁ is 0.14496 on L's ruler” named rulers and numbers neither picture showed; learner, 2026-11-09: “where exactly are those numbers”). One line inside: origin, representation, consumer. A `ⓘ` that computes a length or a number names, in its first words and in the caption line it sits on, the drawn object it measures, and that step's picture labels the object with the number (“0.953: the length of the dark gray line, from Gx to the tip of N” — not “T is 0.953 from Gx ⓘ” over a chain for another line; learner, 2026-11-04).
14. When an older formula is reused (smoothstep, length, source-over), refresh its mechanics right where it is used, in a `ⓘ` tooltip (inline only if it takes a few words). State what goes in and what comes out. A pointer such as “card 8” or “Section 3” is not a refresher: the formula and the source of each of its numbers are on the same screen, in that tooltip. Inside a chain, a line that adds, subtracts or divides by a constant says why (sd − (−0.10): how far sd is past −0.10, where the smoothstep band starts).
15. A claim of change, sameness or shape ("grows", "the same everywhere", "stays straight", "bends", "smooth") is immediately followed by the numbers or the picture that prove it. A claim that two triangles match (the same triangle, or the same shape) names every fact it needs, and the picture marks each one: a square corner and a long side of 1 fit many triangles; the shared corner at S is the third fact, and a square corner with the same corner at S gives the same shape (learner, 2026-11-02: “this is not enough for congruent triangles, they need one more rule”). A length by Lesson 9.1 names its right triangle and that triangle's square corner, so its long side is clear; a side that belongs to two right triangles on one screen says which one the formula uses (10.29: GxF₁ is a short side of △SGxF₁ and the long side of △GxDF₁; learner, 2026-11-09: “GxF1 is not the biggest side in the right triangle”). Show a rate ("slope", "how fast") as the drawn difference between neighbouring samples at a stated step, never as a bare rate such as −5, and show what the rate changes in the picture. Numbers that prove a shape are drawn too (the gap on the picture); an effect word (break, corner, copies) is defined by pointing at it in a picture. A claim that a color change is strong or weak draws channel bars for every color involved and prints every channel's change, not a chosen few. A color-coded display map (slope colors, normal colors) gets a reading figure in the lesson that introduces it: the map with numbered points around the whole shape, the colors stored along one line with every channel drawn as a curve on the same x scale under a slice of the surface, a table from the value to each channel, and one sentence on where the color is plain and where it changes, compared with the previous lesson's map. A distortion is explained by following several labelled points of the shape (its ends, middle, top, bottom) through the whole formula, step by step; each shape word (bends, longer, thinner) is derived from those points' numbers and drawn as a bar between two named points, before and after. A same-factor argument reads “k times as far → k times as wide”, with k computed on both quantities and each quantity drawn as a bracket between its ends; it says when it holds (any triangle, if both ends get the same k) and computes each end's own k when they can differ. When a value has no short formula, give its equation, read it off a drawn chart, and check it in a table. A value the lesson finds (a fold line, also for other dial values) is found in the main flow with reasoning the learner already has: a crossing on a drawn chart whose curve is built from steps they followed, checked just before and just after the crossing. A formula cited from an optional window, or a table of results, is not an explanation. An optional derivation window follows the same rules: every fact it uses is either from the prerequisite lessons or stated with an everyday picture and checked with numbers (a circle's edge at a right angle to its radius: measure the climb over a tiny step), and it never solves for an unknown with algebra the prerequisite lessons do not teach. When an exact closed form can be derived using only math already taught, present its derivation and use it before any numerical search. A numerical search is a fallback when no suitable exact form exists, or a check after an exact result; it finds the value by trying and narrowing one decimal at a time, with zooming rulers and the full numbers of each try. Never let a numerical check replace an exact formula merely because the formula needs a carefully drawn derivation. A stretch factor says what it measures (a length along a stated direction, or an area); an area factor made of two length factors is drawn on one small square of the shape, before and after each factor (1 × 1 → k₁ × 1 → k₁ × k₂), with the shape drawn straight at one scale around it. When the learner asks why something holds, give the proof first, in one or two sentences, then one picture that shows exactly that proof (for “no missing pixels”: every output pixel's read point as a dot on the wallpaper, and a clicked pixel's read point in the middle of its neighbours'), plus a toggle to the way that fails (pushing pixels forward leaves holes). Showing data is not a proof: no walkthroughs, mini-charts or text blocks that do not carry the argument. A claim that a slanted stroke bends is proved by comparing at least two consecutive moves: draw each move’s horizontal and vertical parts and compute right ÷ down (or right ÷ up). A bare “horizontal change per row” or “uneven vertical spacing” is not the explanation.

## Lesson shape

16. One new idea per C lesson, none in a B lesson, one practice per E lesson (the curriculum marks each lesson's type). An idea is a fact or method with its own reason that no earlier lesson taught. A step that needs a second new idea becomes its own C lesson before it: when generating or a fresh review shows one, split at once, without asking (the learner accepts every split in advance, 2026-11-04): renumber the curriculum and every file that names a moved lesson, then say in chat what was split and why. When in doubt, split: more, smaller lessons are preferred, the heavy math may grow by any ratio, and repeating an earlier mechanism in a new place is fine (learner, 2026-11-02). The words and numbers a lesson's idea is stated in (10.27's along and across) are part of that idea, not a second one: the lesson explains them clearly and keeps them (learner, 2026-11-09: “Don't split the along/across concept should be clearly explained in this lesson, otherwise it's getting too granular now”; 10.26 was kept whole the same day). A pointer to a later lesson for a fact (“Lesson 10.5 shows why”) is a promise: a split that moves the fact moves the pointer in the same edit, and no lesson before the fact's lesson may use it (the spin page used the wall's flatness while Lesson 10.4 still pointed to 10.5 for it, 2026-11-03).
17. **Quality of explanation, not quantity (learner, 2026-10-31).** Say each fact once, in the place where it explains best, and make that place clear. An unclear explanation is rewritten; it is never rescued by another block that says it again. Before adding a caption line, table, legend, note, card or section, name what it adds that the same screen does not already show; if nothing, leave it out. A stepper state is its picture and its caption: a table under it only for numbers neither of them can hold, a legend only for marks too small to label. A traced-value table holds only the chain of the lesson's claim, each number once on the screen (not again in the drawing beside it, not restated as “worked out” and “measured”); a contrast case whose numbers differ (an angled view's pixels for a length the lesson says is kept) stays in the step that explains it, never as a bare row (learner, 2026-11-04). A computation the lesson's argument needs (M's numbers from X and the tip of L) is never a recall: it is a step of its own, drawn (legs labelled with their numbers and directions) and explained line by line in the caption, starting from what the three numbers mean; an ⓘ never carries a new computation (learner, 2026-11-08: “explaining easier stuff 10000 times and when it comes to harder stuff just skim through and pretend as if it was mentioned before”). A recall of something earlier (an older formula, an earlier number, a chain, a comparison with an earlier lesson) goes in a `ⓘ` tooltip where it is used, never in a block: the learner may or may not need it, and the lesson stays short. No recap notes. No second section over the same numbers. Short lessons: one concept, then stop. That is why the course has 322 lessons. The idea must be complete after the first two sections. Do not prove side facts (why a curve has no corner, where a peak sits, an exact region shape): a side fact gets one sentence, one picture, or a pointer (a pointer to a later lesson says that this lesson does not need it). Q&A answers stay short and never grow into proof rounds. No warm-up sections: one preview sentence in the hero, then the point. Each later section must add a new object, a new operation, or a new consumer — never restate earlier content. A requested rewrite redesigns the lesson from this rulebook: do not keep the old section skeleton, the old steppers, or proofs already taught in an earlier lesson (point to that lesson in one sentence). Only the rendering engine and the verified numbers may carry over. When a result has an obvious next question (is the fold symmetric? why is one copy squeezed and the other stretched?), answer it in its own step with numbers and a picture. A learner's clarifying question is answered in the lesson's steps; no lesson has a questions section (learner, 2026-11-09: “Yes, every lesson”, after asking that 10.29's turn card be “just part of the stepper in section 1, I don't want "questions" section”). A confusion caused by a wrong or missing name, number or label is fixed where it arose; a fact the steps skipped becomes its own step where it is needed, with its picture drawn on the lesson's real shape at a named pixel and a caption under Rule 0, and the other steps keep their lines (the 2026-11-01 worry, answers that “pollute the rest of the content”, is met by giving an answer its own step, not by adding lines to other steps). The chat gives the short answer first. An existing questions section stays until the learner asks to fold it into the steps; its cards that a fix has made obsolete (the question was about something since changed or removed) are removed (learner, 2026-11-09: “obsolete, please remove”), and a section left empty is deleted. A split keeps a source page's existing cards in the first new page, each pointing to the lesson that now teaches its topic (learner, 2026-11-08: “No answers to my questions … were provided anywhere”). When the learner moves a lesson's cards to a reference sheet (Lessons 10.25–10.27's to `course/reference-3d-geometry-qa.html`, 2026-11-09), the sheet asks each question in its own neutral guide words, never a quote and never as the learner asking, groups the cards by topic, merges two cards on one question into one (their extra lines added, one picture kept), keeps each answer and picture, and the lesson keeps one line with a link per question. A further case of a picture a step already has is a selector on that picture, not a new picture: all of the step follows it, and the first case stays as it was (learner, 2026-11-01). A question about a picture that is right is answered in words; the picture is not rebuilt unless the learner asks (rebuilding a 3D picture was rolled back by the learner, 2026-11-01). An answer's table holds only the objects its question names, under plain direction headings (to the right, toward the bottom of the picture, up; “Along x” failed), and a helper arrow copied from another section without its definition fails (bare rows “x part 1, y part 0, height part −0.349”). Everywhere, a printed 0, 1 or minus says what it means where it is printed: 0, it does not go that way; minus, it goes the opposite way; 1, which walk or choice it comes from. A learner's “why does this shape look like that” question is answered in the lesson with a new picture, not with a chat table: a bend is shown as stairs along the stroke (per row of the picture, how far down and how far sideways it goes, before and after), with a toggle to the case that does not bend. Define every comparison case with a picture before using it, and open it with the question it answers (“does every rim fold?”) (a “straight ramp” is first drawn next to the quarter circle, with h = pixel shown in a table). Keep pictures readable: few, widely spaced lines, each labelled; never a dense web of curves. One idea gets one test surface: do not teach it on a 2D test case and then again in 3D. Two surfaces, two coordinate systems and the length taught twice left the learner unable to connect them; a 2D fact (a quarter turn) is drawn as the flat view of the 3D object, beside it; a side case (a rising surface) appears on the real object (the left rim), not on a second test surface. An explanation made along one special line (under a straight edge) says whether it holds elsewhere (around a rounded corner) and shows in its own step what changes there. New-learning gate: every full lesson adds a genuinely new mechanism, API behavior, math operation, measurement method or implementation step; a recap is brief and serves that step; material that is mostly consolidation becomes a short checkpoint or merges into the next lesson.
18. Required scaffold by lesson type (the curriculum marks each lesson C, B or E).
- **C · concept** (one new idea): visual hero → the idea in pictures, one stepper of at most 5 steps → one traced value with a draggable picture → 1–2 exercises with hidden answers, all with Rule 0's short text. Pick the teaching numbers so no two parts print the same number unless that is the point (rawNormal (1, 0, 1) at a slope of −1 hid which 1 came from the slope and which was chosen). No coding challenge, no Metal exercise, no iOS explorer, no record. Code appears only when the idea is itself a code line: at most 3 lines, with the line table.
- **B · build** (no new idea; it joins the concept lessons before it, named in its curriculum entry): visual hero naming the joined lessons → one recall line per joined idea, each with its `ⓘ` → short code excerpt → Metal exercise, or a Swift exercise for SwiftUI work (the learner writes at least one meaningful function — more than a few lines, joining the joined lessons — in a lab file whose stubs compile; the calling code is prepared: a stitchable caller in that file, an answer-key shader with `static` helpers, and a check view on the chapter screen that draws yours over the key with `.blendMode(.difference)` and `markDifference` paints red where they differ; the page shows the goal picture, the signatures with doc comments, test pixels with target values, the prepared code, and a hidden answer with every number) → the iOS explorer → 1–2 exercises with hidden answers → copyable Markdown record of the joined lessons. If the build needs a new idea, a C lesson comes first.
- **E · engineering** (SwiftUI, architecture, testing, performance): the problem in one short paragraph and one picture → the code with a line table → a check (a test, a preview, or a measurement with its numbers) → 1–2 exercises with hidden answers → copyable Markdown record. Code first and short: the learner is a senior engineer; no math steppers.
- **Exercises, every type.** The correct choice sits first, in the middle or last, varied across exercises, never first by habit (on 2026-11-09, 36 of 48 were first and 2 last; reordered course-wide). An exercise is drawn with the lesson's own pictures. The question shows only the lesson's 3D view (camera, names, colors, labels), with a red marker on what to find or calculate, and no flat drawing beside it (learner, 2026-11-07: “I don't want both 3D and 2D graphics in the question, it just piles up”); the hidden answer shows the same 3D view and the flat drawing its sections used; a 2D-only view (front view, view from above) or a flat drawing of a case never shown in 3D fails when the lesson taught in 3D (learner, 2026-11-07, on 10.19 and 10.20: “exercises diagram not corresponding with anything from the material”, “2D graphics instead of 3D graphics when they were explained”). An answer read off a ruler between ticks (“about 0.93”) is not a question: pick a case the lesson's idea settles. Each exercise tests the lesson's own idea, not a side fact, and never asks back a step whose picture already shows the answer (10.12's “|SGx| and |GxT| stay fixed, but |ST| is free. Must ∠SGxT stay 90°?” repeated Step 2's slider and was called “one of the stupidest questions”, learner 2026-11-05). Use the idea on a new case or where a later lesson needs it; opens with its picture, every named point drawn and labelled; says which code line changed and what that line does, only if one did; says why the question matters without giving the answer, and what the learner does to answer (which facts to read off the picture, which line combines them); and asks one question, the same as its title, a plain full sentence in words the lesson has already used, with 2–4 clickable choices. No “label: question” form. No measure coined for an exercise: “goes right 1 for 1 up” and “right 0.949 for up 0.316” failed; an arrow is described by its named parts. A hint or hidden answer gives the reason in plain words and does not rerun the derivation in its vocabulary: “its quarter turn is (1, 1)” and “seen from the front” failed; any relation an answer states (“more x part, less height part”) is taught in the lesson first and explained where the answer uses it. Each hidden answer draws the reason.
- State when the browser model differs from iOS. Separate what we see from what the framework does internally; label estimates as estimates.
19. Keep each walkthrough state self-contained: repeat the current values beside the formula that consumes them, so the reader never scrolls back to decode a substitution; older values and chains go in a `ⓘ`. A stepper's numbers table, when rule 17 allows one, sits directly under its pictures; beside a side panel taller than the pictures, so pictures and numbers stay on one screen. When a derivation puts one formula into another, show the inserted formula on the same screen, color the inserted part like its source (for sums of squares: one bar per squared number, the sum drawn as the same colored bars end to end, so √(x part² + y part² + height part²) is one bar of three pieces; never write “the first root is not needed” without that picture); for a change of range (× 0.5 + 0.5 from −1…1 to 0…1): number lines on one shared scale, one line per operation, the target range shaded as a zone, dashed lines for where the two ends go, real values marked on every line, and the reason said first (a color channel cannot hold a value below 0); never two lines at different scales, where the learner could not tell what the −1…1 line and the 0…1 line under it were for). A table of values that come from a chain (the color guide's N per point) gives every row an ⓘ with that row's complete chain, the input chains included (the four heights), at enough decimals that the chain rounds to the table's value; its last line says the table rounds (5 decimals for the color guide: with 3, the diagonal points came out 0.711 instead of 0.709), do one move per line named in words, and check every line in a numbers column. When a table's rows belong to different pictures or parts of a picture, heading rows group them by picture (on the wallpaper / in the output / compare). Never use a value that a later section computes (no “from Section 2” inside Section 1): the idea sections use the real glass at a named pixel in a zoomed 3D view (learner, 2026-11-02: “don't use board, but the real-world 3D graphic”; no test surface such as the tilted board), whose numbers are never 0 or 1 and never equal to another number on the same screen, multiplied out with all their digits so every line is exact (0.302 × 0.48 = 0.14496); when a printed value is 1 or −1 (the chosen height part 1), every line names what its number is (x walk height part = slopeX × 1 = (−1) × 1 = −1; N.x = rawNormal.x ÷ normalLength; length² = shadow² + height part², never shadow² + 1²: the bare 1² made the labelled height part look unused) and the section says so once; once a section is in 3D, every arrow is written with three parts ((1, 0, −1), never (1, −1)) (a slope of −1 next to a walk of 1 read as “always 1”; start from a 3-4-5 ramp and show 1 later as one case), and the long chain from the inputs (springs → … → color) lives only in the traced-value section, as cards in reading order, each with its own small picture. Every printed position names its coordinate system, origin and axes (pt from the test picture's top-left corner, y down); a bare “x = 257 pt” is a failure. Every picture names its system in a label inside it (UV, a side view in UV, polar, a made-up test grid, 3D test units; in a 3D picture the first line of the corner box). No “coordinate systems in this lesson” panel (learner, 2026-11-04): each picture, caption, table and printed number names its own system where it is used (a point in UV; an arrow in arrow units, counted from its start; pixels only as a scale). Numbers in brackets can mean different things (a point, an arrow, an arrow in one view, a color); the words just before the brackets say which. Every form the course writes is listed once, with an example read in words, in `course/reference-number-forms.html`; a lesson that adds a form adds its row there, and a lesson links to that page instead of repeating it. The caption of the step that switches system says so; one picture never mixes two systems unless it prints the conversion.

## Verification — non-negotiable, in this order

20. Cold-read audit first, as a total math beginner who knows only arithmetic and the prerequisite lessons: the first sentence, the first two sections, the first formula, the longest stepper state, and every part a revision adds (an answer to a learner question is such a part). Do this pass on every lesson and every revision. Any hesitation means rewrite before technical QA. Working controls and correct arithmetic do not rescue a lesson a beginner cannot follow. Write the cold read down per stepper state: every name, notation and number in its caption and table → its coordinate system → where that state's picture draws and labels it → where the page defined it earlier. Then check the logic: every “so”, “why” and “because” line lists all the facts it needs, each shown earlier or on the same screen; a line carried over from an older version gets the same check. An empty cell means rewrite; the usual ones are a number without a unit (“1”) and a label the picture does not show (“the across leg”, “at W” with no W drawn). Then the fresh review, which also checks Rule 0 (length, plain words, notation, conclusions said once; a fix may be a cut): extract the page with `tools/beginner-review/extract_lesson.py` and give it to a fresh agent with `tools/beginner-review/REVIEWER_PROMPT.md` (an agent that did not write the lesson; never a fork of the writing session). It also lists the lesson's new ideas; more than the type allows is split at once (rule 16). Fix every `blocks` and `slows` item (an item about a side fact is fixed by cutting or shortening it, never by adding a comparison case or a “would be” number: “the hero's whole glass uses 0.06 UV, where the screen would be 0.0866 ÷ 0.06 = 1.443 below Gx” failed, learner, 2026-11-04), run a new fresh agent once more, and save its final list as `reviews/lesson-NN-MM-beginner-review-YYYY-MM-DD.md`.
